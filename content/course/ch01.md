# 1. The Principles of NLP

<p class='subtitle'>The core beliefs and mindset tools the rest of NLP training is built on</p>

## Principle 1: You don't see the world as it is; you see it as you are

<p>Most people walk around thinking they're right and everyone else is wrong. In reality we only see a tiny, filtered fragment of the world. NLP aims to take back control of the internal picture, mainly by understanding the <b>Reticular Activating System (RAS)</b>.</p>

### How the brain filters information

<p>Your nervous system receives about <b>2 million bits</b> of information per second. You can't process all of it, so the brain does three things:</p>

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>Filter</th><th>What it does</th><th>Example</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><b>Delete</b></td><td>Leaves information out of awareness</td><td>Not noticing the air-conditioner hum until someone mentions it</td></tr><tr><td><b>Distort</b></td><td>Changes or misrepresents information</td><td>Two witnesses describe the same car crash completely differently</td></tr><tr><td><b>Generalise</b></td><td>Turns one experience into a rule</td><td>"All men are..." / "I always fail at diets"</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<p>What shapes these filters: <b>Values, beliefs, emotions, behaviours and patterns of behaviour, life experiences, upbringing, and cultural conditioning</b>. These filters stay with us for life unless we "clean them out". If we don't, we keep repeating the same habits and wondering why results don't change.</p>

<p>The full list of filters: <b>Beliefs, values, attitude, mood, attention, stress, meta models, internal dialogue, and even blood sugar</b>. Much of this filtering is linked to the <b>Prefrontal cortex</b>, the planning and attention centre at the front of the brain. What gets through is about <b>4 clusters</b> of information (roughly 64 bits), and around <b>80%</b> of it is visual; the rest is auditory, kinaesthetic, self-talk, smell and taste.</p>

<div class='box extra'><div class='box-title'>Going deeper</div><p><b>Blood sugar and mood:</b> being hungry really does change attention, patience and decisions ("hangry" is a real effect). The once-famous idea that willpower runs on glucose (Gailliot &amp; Baumeister, 2007) has not held up well in larger replications, but regular meals and water still help you stay in a resourceful state.</p>

</div>

### The Reticular Activating System (RAS)

<p>The RAS works like a <b>Sonar</b>, scanning the world and bringing into awareness whatever is most important to you. If you're not conscious of your filters, the RAS will keep finding evidence for whatever you're already focused on, good or bad.</p>

### The numbers (as taught)

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>Item</th><th>Figure often quoted</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Information hitting the nervous system</td><td>2,000,000 bits per second</td></tr><tr><td>Share that is visual</td><td>About 80%</td></tr><tr><td>Original NLP view (1960s–70s) of what we consciously hold</td><td>5 to 9 chunks (7 ± 2)</td></tr><tr><td>Current view</td><td><b>4 chunks</b> = 64 "bits" of information</td></tr><tr><td>Share of reality you perceive</td><td>64 ÷ 2,000,000 = <b>0.0032%</b></td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<p>"7 ± 2" comes from George Miller's 1956 paper <i>The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two</i>. The "4 chunks" figure comes from Nelson Cowan (2001). Both are real research.</p>

<p>The "2 million bits" figure is a popular NLP number, not a precise measurement. Other sources quote ~11 million bits/sec of sensory input versus roughly 40–50 bits/sec of conscious processing. The point (we consciously process a tiny fraction) is sound; the exact numbers vary by source.</p>

</div>

<blockquote class='quote'>"Where focus goes, energy flows, and results show."</blockquote>

<p>Try this: you probably weren't aware of the feeling of your feet in your shoes, your pulse, or background sounds until they were mentioned. They were always there; your brain was deleting them because they didn't seem important.</p>

<div class='box fact'><div class='box-title'>Fact check</div><ul><li>The arithmetic (64 ÷ 2,000,000) gives <b>0.0032%</b>, about 3 in every 100,000 bits.</li></ul></div>

## Principle 2: There is no such thing as "I don't know"

<p>People often answer "I don't know" because they're afraid of what the real answer might be, often tied to a limiting belief, emotion or pattern. In this environment, "I don't know" is not accepted as the end of the conversation.</p>

<blockquote class='quote'>"I know you don't know, but if you <i>did</i> know, what would the answer be?"</blockquote>

<p>This is a <b>Linguistic possibility</b>: the sentence presupposes that an answer exists, so the mind goes looking for it. People usually come up with an answer surprisingly quickly.</p>

<div class='box extra'><div class='box-title'>Going deeper</div><p>In NLP language patterns this works through <b>Presupposition</b> (the answer is assumed to exist) and an <b>"as if" frame</b> (acting as if you already knew). Similar coaching versions: "What would you say if you did know?" or "Just guess. What's the first thing that comes to mind?"</p>

</div>

## Principle 3: Proximity equals power

<p>We are <b>Tribal creatures</b>. A deep part of the brain is driven by survival, and fitting in with the group feels safe. We unconsciously model the behaviour of those around us (linked to <b>Mirror neurons</b>, covered in depth later in Communication Mastery).</p>

<p>Story: at Brisbane airport at 9:30 am, the practitioner watched three miners in high-vis drive off with energy drinks, pies and sausage rolls. Imagine being the one in the back seat trying to eat a salad. Even if your friends are supportive, different habits create friction.</p>

<div class='box fact'><div class='box-title'>Fact check</div><ul><li>In the actual book (Chapter 1, Ramadi), the friendly-fire incident involved SEALs, US Army units and friendly Iraqi soldiers; an Iraqi soldier was killed and a SEAL wounded.</li></ul></div>

<div class='box key'><div class='box-title'>Key Takeaway</div><p>The people you spend time with can empower you or disempower you. <b>Peer pressure is real, so choose your peers wisely.</b></p>

</div>

<div class='box extra'><div class='box-title'>Going deeper</div><p>Research support: the Framingham Heart Study analyses (Christakis &amp; Fowler, 2007–2008) found that obesity, smoking and even happiness spread through social networks, up to three degrees of separation. Jim Rohn's popular line "You are the average of the five people you spend the most time with" expresses the same idea.</p>

</div>

## Principle 4: Whatever you believe is true, is true (for you)

<blockquote class='quote'>"Whether you think you can, or you think you can't, you're right." (attributed to Henry Ford)</blockquote>

<p>There are no "good" or "bad" beliefs, only <b>Empowering</b> and <b>Disempowering</b> beliefs. A belief doesn't have to be real, right or factual; it is true for you simply because you believe it (e.g. flat-earthers). It stays that way until the belief is changed or an experience breaks it. You will audit your beliefs later in NLP training.</p>

<div class='box extra'><div class='box-title'>Going deeper</div><p>Related psychology: the <b>Self-fulfilling prophecy</b> (Robert Merton), the <b>Placebo effect</b>, and <b>Confirmation bias</b> (we notice evidence that supports what we already believe, which is the RAS at work).</p>

</div>

## Principle 5: Success in any area requires obsession

<p>"Everything in moderation" is described as the battle cry of mediocrity. this is his own principle rather than classic NLP. You don't want a <i>moderate</i> amount of health, money or love; you want them in abundance. The only reason people seek work-life balance, he argues, is that their work isn't fulfilling.</p>

<p>Without positive obsession you end up "majoring in minor things". Les Brown's warning: go through life casually and you'll become a casualty. Double and triple down on personal growth.</p>

## Principle 6: The 90/10 rule

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>Victim mindset</th><th>Growth mindset (what NLP teaches)</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Life is <b>90% circumstance</b>, 10% choice</td><td>Life is <b>10% circumstance</b>, 90% choice</td></tr><tr><td>"I had a bad upbringing / got laid off, there's nothing I can do"</td><td>"What do I get to do about it? How do I use this rather than let it use me?"</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<div class='box extra'><div class='box-title'>Going deeper</div><p>A similar idea is Charles Swindoll's well-known saying that life is 10% what happens to you and 90% how you react to it. "Growth mindset" is Carol Dweck's term (<i>Mindset</i>, 2006).</p>

</div>

## Principle 7: How you do anything is how you do everything

<p>We are creatures of habit. NLP training's <b>"30-year rule"</b>: by age 30, about 95% of a person's neurological capacity is preset, so your future becomes a well-rehearsed past. Repeated behaviour wires neurons together until you run on <b>Autopilot</b>, called <b>Automaticity</b> in behavioural change. If you show up deliberately now, you set a precedent for showing up deliberately in the future. How you engage with learning is how you engage with life.</p>

</div>


<div class='box fact'><div class='box-title'>Fact check</div><ul><li>The "95% preset by 30" figure is a motivational claim, not an established scientific measurement. Neuroscience shows the brain stays <b>plastic</b> (able to rewire) throughout life, though habits do become more ingrained over time. The underlying principle, "neurons that fire together wire together", comes from Donald Hebb (1949).</li></ul></div>

## Principle 9: You are responsible for everything in your life

<blockquote class='quote'>Responsibility = Response + Ability</blockquote>

<p>The practitioner prefers "responsibility" to "fault". <b>Nothing in life is your fault, but everything is your responsibility.</b> Responsibility doesn't mean you caused it; it means you have the ability to choose your response (even if the response is to do nothing). This links back to the 90/10 rule.</p>

### Extreme Ownership: the blue-on-blue story

<p>Book: <b><i>Extreme Ownership</i></b> by <b>Jocko Willink</b> and <b>Leif Babin</b>, former Navy SEAL officers who led in Iraq.</p>

<ul><li>NLP training says it costs about <b>$3.1 million</b> to create a Navy SEAL. Training includes <b>Hell Week</b> (David Goggins, author of <i>Can't Hurt Me</i>, went through it three times).</li><li>Jocko, in command, has several teams in the field and only one heavy-armour rescue unit. Team A reports being pinned down; then Team F also reports heavy fire and a man killed.</li><li>Jocko notices the radio chatter follows a pattern and orders Team F to stand down. The firing stops: Teams A and F had been fighting <b>Each other</b>, a <b>"blue on blue"</b> (friendly fire), because Team F had the wrong coordinates.</li><li>At the debrief, everyone stands up one by one to take responsibility, from the shooter up the chain of command. Finally Jocko says the responsibility is his alone: as the commander, he should have communicated clearly and anticipated it.</li><li>Every SEAL team member must be able to take ownership at any moment, from <b>Role</b>, not ego.</li></ul>

</div>

<div class='box key'><div class='box-title'>Key Takeaway</div><p>Are you having "blue on blues" in your own life, getting in your own way? Stop blaming others (ex-partners, family, colleagues): blaming gives away your power. <b>Own the good, the bad and the ugly</b>, because then you have the ability to change it.</p>

</div>
