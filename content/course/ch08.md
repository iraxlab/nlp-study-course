# 8. The 4 Pillars of Outstanding Communication

<p class='subtitle'>Communication Mastery, part 1</p>

<p><b>Life is a team sport.</b> Every goal requires working with people: aligning, motivating and supporting them, all of which are communication challenges. Communication is the bedrock of success, and <b>If you can't communicate, you can't coach</b>, which is why it's taught first.</p>

<p>The Communication Mastery module covers: the 4 pillars, <b>Rapport</b>, <b>Mirroring and matching</b>, <b>Chunking</b>, <b>Metaphors</b>, <b>Agreement frames</b> and <b>Reframes</b>.</p>

<blockquote class='quote'>"Everything is possible in the presence of rapport."</blockquote>

<p>If you're not creating change with a client, the most likely reason is a lack of rapport. The 4 pillars are a life principle rather than a classic NLP tool.</p>

Awareness</b></td><td>Be aware of the communication environment, yourself and the other person. You can have the right conversation at the wrong time.</td></tr><tr><td><b>2. ## Pillar 1: Awareness

<ul><li><b>The client Clinton's 3 questions</b> before taking offence: Have they slept? Have they eaten? Is something going on in their personal life?</li><li>Timing: as a young man the practitioner unloaded problems on his parents the moment they got home from work.</li><li><b>Oprah</b>: be aware of the energy you bring into the room.</li><li>Context matters: the bedroom, a coffee shop, a boardroom and the couch call for different conversations.</li></ul>

## Pillar 2: Authentic listening

<p>Most people listen waiting to catch the other person out. Instead, have a conversation with a <b>Centre, not two sides</b>: work <i>with</i> the person to understand them rather than against them to prove them wrong.</p>

## Pillar 3: Suspending

<ul><li>Suspend the internal dialogue; this may slow the conversation down, and that's fine.</li><li>NLP training notes that in Japanese culture long pauses before responding are normal.</li><li>You have two ears and one mouth; use them in that proportion.</li><li>Hear them, try to understand, process how you feel, and ask more questions before speaking.</li></ul>

## Pillar 4: Speaking your truth

<ul><li>If you do the first three well, <b>You may not need to speak at all</b>. Needing the last word is ego, significance and validation.</li><li>Most of the time people simply want to <b>Feel heard</b>. Get that right and most arguments disappear.</li><li>The other person doesn't have to agree. You don't have to make them wrong to be right; you can both be right and still disagree.</li><li>Don't say what you think they want to hear. Speak from the heart, respectfully.</li><li>Don't demand that others listen this way, but in an argument you can ask: "Are you listening right now to respond, or to genuinely understand me?"</li></ul>

<blockquote class='quote'>"The single biggest problem in communication is the illusion that it has taken place."</blockquote>

<p>Since we only perceive a tiny fraction of reality, people are often "worlds apart" while believing they understand each other. That we communicate at all, largely thanks to non-verbal cues, is something to celebrate.</p>

<div class='box extra'><div class='box-title'>Going deeper</div><p>"Seek first to understand, then to be understood" is <b>Habit 5</b> of Stephen Covey's <i>The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People</i> (1989), the same idea as authentic listening.</p>

<p>"Suspending" comes from <b>David Bohm's dialogue</b> work and William Isaacs' <i>Dialogue and the Art of Thinking Together</i>, where the four practices are listening, respecting, suspending and voicing. The 4 pillars closely mirror this.</p>

<p><b>Active listening</b> techniques (Carl Rogers): paraphrase what you heard, reflect feelings, ask open questions, and summarise before responding.</p>

<p>The "what you know / know you don't know / don't know you don't know" idea is often shown as the <b>Johari Window</b> or the <b>Conscious competence</b> ladder.</p>

<p>On non-verbal communication: Albert Mehrabian's "7% words, 38% tone, 55% body language" is often quoted, but it only applies to messages about feelings and attitudes when words and tone conflict, not to all communication.</p>
