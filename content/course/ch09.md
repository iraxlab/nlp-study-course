# 9. Sensory Acuity

<p class='subtitle'>Communication Mastery: reading the subtle signals people don't say out loud</p>

<p><b>Sensory acuity</b> is a fancy term for an <b>Expanded level of awareness</b>: the ability to notice small changes in another person's body, face, skin and breathing. It's an almost "sixth sense" that matters when building rapport and when doing change work, because clients usually <b>Won't tell you</b> what they don't consciously understand. You have to read it from their body language.</p>

<blockquote class='quote'>"You must be able to read people in order to lead them."</blockquote>

## Why it works: the subconscious mind

<p>Beneath the conscious mind is the <b>Subconscious mind</b>, whose one overarching function is to <b>Keep you alive</b>. It runs the body's essential systems and many facial and physical expressions. So when someone makes a deep subconscious change, it shows up in their face and body before (or without) them saying anything.</p>

<div class='box key'><div class='box-title'>Key Takeaway</div><p><b>Do not judge or interpret.</b> It doesn't matter whether a change is positive or negative. The skill is simply to notice <b>Subtle shifts</b>, and changes in the person compared with their own baseline.</p>

</div>

## What to watch: the signals

<p>Besides overall <b>Posture</b>, NLP training teaches these cues. Each one is a scale between two ends:</p>

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>Signal</th><th>Notice whether it is...</th><th>Examples</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><b>1. Skin colour</b></td><td>Light / flushed ↔ dark / grey</td><td>Blushing on the cheeks, neck and chest (raised heart rate, embarrassment or attraction); some people get a rash or hives when nervous. Drained or struggling = greyish; energised (after a workout) = bright.</td></tr><tr><td><b>2. Skin tonus</b> (tension)</td><td>Tight ↔ loose / soft</td><td>Peak state (e.g. just been for a run) = tight. Sleepy, lethargic, disengaged = soft.</td></tr><tr><td><b>3. Skin shine</b></td><td>Shiny ↔ dry</td><td>Mentioned in NLP training summary alongside colour and tone.</td></tr><tr><td><b>4. Lower lip size</b></td><td>Full / pronounced ↔ thin / contracted</td><td>Changes person to person and moment to moment; the upper lip shows less change.</td></tr><tr><td><b>5. Pupils</b></td><td>Dilated ↔ contracted ("fixated")</td><td>Affected by light (moving between rooms) but also by thinking and visualising.</td></tr><tr><td><b>6. Pulse / heart rate</b></td><td>Fast ↔ slow</td><td>Easiest to see at the neck when close to the client. Milton Erickson could reportedly see pulses change during hypnotic inductions.</td></tr><tr><td><b>7. Breathing</b></td><td>Deep, low ↔ high, shallow, quick</td><td>Deep (diaphragmatic) breaths = chest rising and falling; nervous or anxious = shortness of breath, quick, high, shallow breathing.</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<div class='box extra'><div class='box-title'>Going deeper</div><p><b>Calibration</b> is the classic NLP name for this skill: learning one person's specific signals for specific states (e.g. how <i>this</i> client looks when they say "yes" vs. when they mean "no"). You first establish their <b>Baseline</b>, then watch for <b>Changes from it</b>. Cues only mean something relative to that person's baseline.</p>

<p>These outward signs are sometimes called <b>BMIRs</b>: Behavioural Manifestations of Internal Representations. A person's internal state (thoughts, pictures, feelings) shows up in their physiology.</p>

<p><b>Pacing and leading</b>: you <b>Read</b> the person (sensory acuity), <b>Match / mirror</b> them to build rapport (pacing), then <b>Lead</b> them to a new state. Sensory acuity is how you check whether they are following you.</p>

<p>In change work, sensory acuity is your <b>Test</b>: if a technique has worked, the person's physiology when they think about the old problem should look different from before.</p>

<p>Wider research: Paul Ekman's <b>Facial Action Coding System</b> (FACS) maps facial muscle movements, and his work on <b>Micro-expressions</b> (fleeting expressions lasting a fraction of a second) supports the idea that emotion leaks into the face.</p>
