# 11. Chunking

<p class='subtitle'>Communication Mastery: controlling the level of the conversation</p>

<blockquote class='quote'>"Whoever controls the level of the conversation controls the conversation."</blockquote>

<blockquote class='quote'>"The meaning of your communication is the response it elicits."</blockquote>

<p>If someone responds negatively, the message they received was negative, whatever you intended. It's hard to communicate well, but you still have the <b>Ability to respond</b> (responsibility, Chapter 1). The practitioner: if he says something loving to the client and it lands badly, he lacked awareness of how to connect with her.</p>

<div class='box key'><div class='box-title'>Key Takeaway</div><p>Two-way extreme ownership: the <b>Speaker</b> owns the response their communication gets; the <b>Listener</b> owns how they choose to respond (nobody can force you to feel anything). When both people take ownership, you get a powerful relationship. When neither does, you get "I didn't mean it like that" vs. "It's how you made me feel".</p>

</div>

## What chunking is

<p><b>Chunking</b> shifts the level of a conversation, either to create agreement and connection, or to drill into detail.</p>

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>Direction</th><th>Moves the conversation to...</th><th>Questions to ask</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><b>Chunk UP</b></td><td>A broader, more abstract level (the big picture, purpose, intention)</td><td>"<b>For what purpose?</b>" · "<b>What's the (higher) intention?</b>" · "<b>What is this an example of?</b>"</td></tr><tr><td><b>Chunk DOWN</b></td><td>Greater specificity: the details and minutiae</td><td>"<b>What or whom specifically?</b>" · "<b>What's an example of this?</b>"</td></tr><tr><td><b>Chunk ACROSS</b> (lateral)</td><td>Another example at the same level, or a different subject</td><td>"<b>What's another example of this?</b>"</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<div class='box key'><div class='box-title'>Key Takeaway</div><p><b>Chunk up = more agreement and engagement. Chunk down = more conflict (and more precision).</b> Almost everyone agrees at the top level; disagreement lives in the details. NLP training claims close to 90% of communication conflict comes from people talking at <b>Different levels</b>.</p>

</div>

## Worked examples

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>Level</th><th>Push-ups</th><th>Ferrari</th><th>Apples</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Highest</td><td>Survival</td><td>Expression / living / thriving</td><td>Survival / sustenance</td></tr><tr><td>Up</td><td>Health</td><td>Movement → travel → transport</td><td>Nutrition → eating → food</td></tr><tr><td>Up</td><td>Exercise</td><td>Car</td><td>Fruit and vegetables</td></tr><tr><td><b>Start</b></td><td><b>Push-ups</b></td><td><b>Ferrari</b></td><td><b>Apples</b></td></tr><tr><td>Down</td><td>Wide-grip, tricep, diamond push-ups</td><td>California T, red, two-door</td><td>Red or green, variety, ripe or rotten</td></tr><tr><td>Down</td><td>Muscle groups, sets and reps</td><td>Rims, V8 or V12 engine</td><td>Where sourced, weight, price</td></tr><tr><td>Lowest</td><td>Cells and individual muscle fibres</td><td>Individual components</td><td>Seeds, cells</td></tr><tr><td>Across</td><td>Sit-ups, weights, dancing, Zumba, walking</td><td>Other cars (Mustang, Toyota) or other transport (train, bus, bicycle, skateboard)</td><td>Pears, bananas, oranges</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<p><b>Different chunk-up paths reveal different values.</b> Chunk up on a Ferrari through <i>transport</i> and a $5,000 car does the same job. Chunk up through <i>status symbol → significance → ego / accomplishment / success</i> and it's a completely different decision. (Links to the 7 Human Needs.)</p>

## Using chunking in real conversations

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>Situation</th><th>Move</th><th>Example</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>A generalisation ("You do this <b>All</b> the time")</td><td>Chunk <b>Down</b></td><td>"Can you give me a specific example of when I did that?" Generalisations remove context and can't really be acknowledged.</td></tr><tr><td>Vague feedback at work</td><td>Chunk <b>Down</b></td><td>"What specific feedback, and who specifically said what?"</td></tr><tr><td>Vague agreement ("We're on the same page")</td><td>Chunk <b>Down</b></td><td>"What specifically are we on the same page about?"</td></tr><tr><td>Conflict over details (A vs. B)</td><td>Chunk <b>Up</b></td><td>"Rather than A or B, let's remember why we're doing this in the first place."</td></tr><tr><td>Family argument</td><td>Chunk <b>Up</b></td><td>"It doesn't matter who's right or wrong; we're one family, let's move on."</td></tr></tbody></table></div>


### Why politicians and master communicators stay high-level

<p>Statements like "We've got to put people back to work" or "build a strong economy" are high abstractions everyone agrees with. Detailed plans can be criticised; ideas can't. When pushed for details they may avoid them with a <b>Lateral chunk</b> (changing the subject).</p>

### A good meeting flow

<ol><li><b>Start specific</b>: the agenda. Anything to add?</li><li><b>Chunk up</b>: the vision, mission and direction. Everyone contributes and agrees.</li><li><b>Chunk down</b>: next steps, action items and delegation.</li></ol>

## Know your own default

<div class='box extra'><div class='box-title'>Going deeper</div><p>In NLP this model is called the <b>Hierarchy of Ideas</b> (or chunking ladder), widely taught by Tad James and others. Chunking <b>Across</b> is really chunking up one level and back down to a different example.</p>

<p>Chunking up uses vague, abstract language, like the <b>Milton Model</b> (great for agreement, motivation and hypnosis). Chunking down uses precise questions, like the <b>Meta Model</b> (great for clarity and challenging generalisations such as "always", "never", "everyone").</p>

<p>"The meaning of communication is the response you get" is one of the classic <b>NLP presuppositions</b>, along with "the map is not the territory" and "there is no failure, only feedback".</p>

<p>In <b>Negotiation</b>, chunking up to shared interests is the core idea of <i>Getting to Yes</i> (Fisher &amp; Ury, 1981): focus on <b>Interests</b> (why), not <b>Positions</b> (what). The client Sinek's <i>Start With Why</i> is another chunk-up approach.</p>

<p>In <b>Coaching</b>: chunk up to find a client's deeper values and motivation ("What will that give you?"), then chunk down to the first realistic step (Criteria for Creating Outcomes, Chapter 6).</p>

<p><b>Ethics</b>: about "manipulating" conversations. Used honestly, chunking builds genuine agreement; used to dodge responsibility (like the dishes), it damages trust and rapport.</p>

</div>

<p>The "90% of conflict" figure is NLP training's estimate, not a research statistic.</p>
