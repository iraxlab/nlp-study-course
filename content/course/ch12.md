# 12. Metaphors

<p class='subtitle'>Communication Mastery: stories that slip past resistance</p>

<p>Two common frustrations: people <b>Ask</b> for help, then <b>Resist</b> it when you give it; and loved ones ignore your advice, then hear the same thing from someone else months later and act as if they discovered fire. ("A prophet is not accepted in his own town.") The point: you have to deliver <b>The right message, in the right way, at the right time</b>, like the right key unlocking a door. Metaphors do this.</p>

## Analogy vs. metaphor

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th></th><th>Analogy</th><th>Metaphor</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>What it does</td><td>Links something the person doesn't understand to something they <b>Do</b> understand</td><td>A story with a <b>Deeper meaning</b> that helps people transcend their limitations</td></tr><tr><td>Awareness</td><td><b>Conscious</b>: the person knows you're comparing</td><td><b>Subconscious</b>: works "under the radar"; the person isn't aware what you're doing</td></tr><tr><td>Best for</td><td>Education, training, faster learning</td><td>Change work: getting past resistance to an insight</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<p>Metaphors require <b>A lot of rapport</b> and <b>A lot of patience</b>: most coaches just want to tell people the answer.</p>

<blockquote class='quote'>"Tell me and I will forget. Show me and I may remember. Let me experience it for myself and it will become my truth."</blockquote>

<blockquote class='quote'>"Never tell a story without a point, and never make a point without telling a story." (Les Brown)</blockquote>

## How to build a metaphor: the model

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>Step</th><th>What to do</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><b>1. Present state</b></td><td>What's the client's problem or challenge, as they've expressed it?</td></tr><tr><td><b>2. Desired state</b></td><td>What outcome do they want?</td></tr><tr><td><b>3. The "aha" moment</b></td><td>Ask yourself: <b>"What does this person need to know, such that when they know it, this won't be a problem anymore?"</b></td></tr><tr><td><b>4. Chunk up</b></td><td>"<b>What is this an example of?</b>" Find the bigger pattern behind the problem.</td></tr><tr><td><b>5. Chunk across</b></td><td>"<b>What's another example of that?</b>" Move to a different context, ideally one the client is <b>Interested and passionate</b> about (we only engage with stories about things we care about).</td></tr><tr><td><b>6. Tell the story</b></td><td>Deliver it with <b>No conscious connection</b> to their problem, then <b>Move on</b> with the conversation.</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<div class='box key'><div class='box-title'>Key Takeaway</div><p><b>Never explain the metaphor.</b> You'll be tempted to finish with "...and that's why you need to drink water!" Resist. If you explain it, you destroy its power and you've just told a long-winded analogy. The best metaphors are ones the person never realises are happening.</p>

<p>The aha may come instantly ("Hold on, what if I just did this?"), or days later ("I was thinking about our conversation..."), or not at all. That's fine.</p>

</div>

## Example: the client and the car

<p><b>Present state</b>: stepdad the client (plumber, AFL fan, "man's man") has back pain and drinks no water ("there's water in coffee and beer"). Telling him directly to drink water met resistance. <b>Aha needed</b>: your body needs regular maintenance, like any machine. <b>Interest</b>: cars.</p>

<p><b>The metaphor</b>: in a later conversation the practitioner told a (slightly embellished) story about his car breaking down on the way to an important meeting. The roadside mechanic asked when it was last serviced (years ago), when he last topped up the water and checked the oil (never; "I just drive it from A to B"). The mechanic explained that most people are too busy to look after their car, so it breaks down; top up the water weekly, or even daily. The client was fully engaged and appalled. The practitioner left it there, so the client could draw his own meaning.</p>

## Metaphors are everywhere: The Lion King

<p>Watch films again and you'll see layers you missed as a child. <i>The Lion King</i> contains the <b>Hero's Journey</b>: an heir who refuses his responsibility, the death of the good king, light vs. shadow ("everything the light touches"), friends who just want an easy life (Timon and Pumbaa), a partner who calls him back to responsibility, confronting fears and doing inner work, and the fire that burns away the past before new life (the circle of life). <i>The Matrix</i> works the same way. The best meanings are the ones you find yourself.</p>

Sample ideas are below; try your own first.</p>

It ran dry on the last lap.</td></tr><tr><td>"I don't know what I want to do with my life."</td><td>Clarity comes from trying things, not from thinking</td><td><b>Fashion</b>: a stylist doesn't find their look in a catalogue; they try things on, keep what fits, and their style evolves.</td></tr><tr><td>"I need a hard conversation but don't want to have it."</td><td>Small problems ignored become big, expensive ones</td><td><b>DIY</b>: a tiny roof leak left for a season, "too awkward to get up there", turned into a rotten ceiling and a huge repair bill.</td></tr><tr><td>"I keep procrastinating on starting my business; it's overwhelming."</td><td>You don't need the whole plan, just the next step</td><td><b>Interior design</b>: a full renovation looked impossible until the designer said "just paint one wall this weekend". That wall set the palette for the whole house.</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<div class='box extra'><div class='box-title'>Going deeper</div><p><b>Milton Erickson</b> was the master of therapeutic metaphor ("teaching tales"). A famous example: with a terminally ill florist in pain, he talked at length about how a tomato plant grows comfortably, day by day. The patient reported reduced pain, though it was never directly mentioned.</p>

<p><b>Isomorphic metaphor</b> (David Gordon, <i>Therapeutic Metaphors</i>, 1978): the story's characters and events should <b>Match the structure</b> of the client's situation (same kind of problem, obstacles and relationships) so the subconscious maps one onto the other, then the story resolves in the way you want the client's situation to resolve.</p>

<p><b>Nested loops</b>: an advanced technique of opening several stories inside each other, delivering the key message in the middle, then closing the loops in reverse order.</p>

<p><b>Clean Language</b> (David Grove): instead of supplying metaphors, the coach explores the client's <i>own</i> metaphors ("And what kind of 'stuck' is that stuck?"). Useful because clients already describe problems in metaphor ("I'm hitting a wall", "I'm drowning").</p>

<p><i>Metaphors We Live By</i> (Lakoff &amp; Johnson, 1980) showed that everyday thinking is built on metaphor (e.g. "argument is war": attack a position, defend a claim).</p>

<p>The <b>Hero's Journey</b> comes from Joseph Campbell's <i>The Hero with a Thousand Faces</i> (1949): departure (call to adventure, refusal), initiation (trials, the ordeal) and return (bringing back the reward), which is why so many films feel familiar.</p>
