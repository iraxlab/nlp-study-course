# 13. Frames

<p class='subtitle'>Communication Mastery: managing the frame of reference</p>

<p>A <b>Frame</b> is the lens through which we view and interpret reality. Like chunking, managing frames is how you <b>Control, own or influence a conversation</b>. Imagine everyone has a photo frame (or kaleidoscope) in front of their eyes: whatever you put in the frame is what they see. Frames can <b>Limit</b> or <b>Expand</b> our options.</p>

## Frames are everywhere

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>Example</th><th>How the frame changes the meaning</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><b>Governments and media</b> </td><td>Recorded during COVID: restrictions framed as "surviving a crisis" and "protecting the elderly" were widely accepted; the same rules with no frame would have caused outrage. The practitioner also points to wartime propaganda and how the public idea of "terrorist" shifted over the decades.</td></tr><tr><td><b>Price</b></td><td>A $1,000 handbag marked down to $500 with no explanation feels suspicious; with a big red <b>SALE</b> sticker it feels like a bargain.</td></tr><tr><td><b>Stock market</b></td><td>Rising prices are framed as "everyone doing well" (though shares are more expensive); falling prices are framed as a "bloodbath" rather than "everything's on sale".</td></tr><tr><td><b>Everyday phrases</b></td><td>"It's not a big deal, but..." → now it feels like a big deal. "No offence, but..." → you know you're about to be offended.</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<p>This chapter covers the <b>Four frames</b>. Reframes (two types) are covered in Chapter 19.</p>

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>Frame</th><th>Purpose</th><th>Key words / question</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><b>1. Agreement Frame</b></td><td>Find common ground instead of focusing on differences</td><td>"I <b>Appreciate</b>, and..." · "I <b>Respect</b>, and..." · "I <b>Agree</b>, and..."</td></tr><tr><td><b>2. ## 1. The Agreement Frame

<p>Avoid <b>"I understand"</b>. People say it when they don't, and the other person thinks: "If you understood, we'd have moved on already." "I understand, but..." causes head-butting. Use one of these instead:</p>

<blockquote class='quote'>"I appreciate..., and..." · "I respect..., and..." · "I agree..., and..."</blockquote>

<p><b>How can you say "I agree" when you don't?</b> <b>Chunk up</b> to something you genuinely agree with, then give your view. Example (choosing which project to invest in): "I <b>Appreciate</b> that you want to make the best decision for the company, <b>And</b> that's why I believe this project is the best decision." Agree → chunk up → state your point.</p>

<div class='box extra'><div class='box-title'>Going deeper</div><p>NLP teaches that <b>"but" erases</b> what came before it ("You did great, but..." → people only remember the criticism). <b>"And"</b> keeps both parts alive. That's why the frame uses "I agree, <i>and</i>...". Notice that some of NLP training's own example sentences slip back into "though" and "but"; aim for "and".</p>

</div>

## 2. The Purpose Frame

<p>People often have a desire, chunk down to one specific way of getting it, and get fixated on that detail. Ask <b>"For what purpose?"</b> or <b>"What do you want?"</b> to find the real outcome; there's often an easier route to it.</p>


### Example: the departing team member

<p>A team member listed complaints about changes at work. The practitioner asked what outcome she wanted; she couldn't answer. It turned out she already had another job and wanted a reason to leave. Asking about purpose revealed the real issue quickly.</p>

<div class='box key'><div class='box-title'>Key Takeaway</div><p>Most people are so focused on details and "ticking boxes" that they forget what they're doing it for. <b>Ask what they want, not what they don't like.</b></p>

</div>

## 3. The What If Frame

<p>When someone says "I can't", ask <b>"What would happen if you did?"</b> and keep asking. Most people never look at the worst case, or at how easily they could handle it.</p>

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>Business fear</th><th>Training with kids</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>"If I do this I could lose my whole business!"<br>What if you did? → I'd be bankrupt.<br>What if you were? → I'd have nothing.<br>Then what? → I'd get a job.<br>Then what? → I'd make money and get back to normal.<br>→ <b>So even the worst case is manageable.</b></td><td>"I can't train, I've got the kids."<br>What if you did? → I'd find a sitter or bring them.<br>Could you ask grandparents? → Yes.<br>What if you brought them? → They'd run around.<br>What if you gave them a book or iPad? → It wouldn't be great.<br>Would you still get the workout done? → Yes.<br>→ <b>A workout that isn't perfect beats no workout.</b></td></tr></tbody></table></div>

## 4. The Positively Stated Frame

<p>Ask a client what they want and they'll often say what they <b>Don't</b> want. Turn the negative into a <b>Positive IR</b> (internal representation). <b>Say it how you want it.</b></p>

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>Client says (negative)</th><th>Coach asks</th><th>Positive outcome</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>"I don't want to be in debt."</td><td>How much debt? (≈ $50k) So if you made an extra $50k, you could pay it off?</td><td>Make an extra $50k</td></tr><tr><td>"I don't want her to whinge and criticise me."</td><td>If she wasn't doing that, what would she be doing?</td><td>"I want her to support me."</td></tr><tr><td>"I don't want to be broke anymore."</td><td>If you aren't broke, how will you be instead?</td><td>"I want to be wealthy / rich."</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<p>Process: <b>Hear the negative → ask how it would be if it were positive → get the positive → get agreement on it.</b> (Links to criterion 1 of Creating Outcomes: "stated in the positive".)</p>

<div class='box key'><div class='box-title'>Key Takeaway</div><p>The four frames (Agreement, Purpose, What If, Positively Stated) should become <b>Go-to habits</b> rather than scripts: use them with clients, at work, and at home.</p>

</div>

<div class='box extra'><div class='box-title'>Going deeper</div><p><b>The framing effect</b> (Tversky &amp; Kahneman, 1981): people chose differently when the same medical outcome was described as "200 people will be saved" vs. "400 people will die". Same facts, different frame, different decision. Kahneman later won the Nobel Prize in economics, partly for this work.</p>

<p><b>Anchoring</b>: the original price on a sale tag acts as an anchor that makes the new price feel cheap.</p>

<p><b>Pre-framing</b>: setting the frame before you speak ("This might sound odd at first, and..."). "No offence, but..." is a pre-frame that backfires.</p>

<p>Why positive framing matters: the mind has to picture something to process it. Tell someone "don't think of a pink elephant" and they picture it. Psychologist Daniel Wegner's "white bear" studies (1987) showed that trying to suppress a thought makes it come back more often.</p>

<p>The What If frame is similar to <b>Fear-setting</b> (Tim Ferriss) and the Stoic practice of <b>Premeditatio malorum</b> (imagining the worst so it loses its power), and to <b>Decatastrophising</b> in CBT.</p>

<p>A family custody dispute is a textbook example of <b>Interests vs. positions</b> from <i>Getting to Yes</i>: the positions (who gets custody) clashed; the underlying interest (contact with the child) was easy to meet.</p>

<p>Other classic NLP frames you may meet later: the <b>Outcome frame</b> (focus on what you want), <b>Ecology frame</b>, <b>Evidence frame</b> ("How will you know?"), <b>As-if frame</b>, and <b>Backtrack frame</b> (summarising the other person's words back to them).</p>

</div>
