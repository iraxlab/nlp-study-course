# 16. Linguistic Presuppositions

<p class='subtitle'>Communication Mastery: the hidden assumptions inside people's words</p>

## The power of language

<p>We often don't know what a client is thinking or feeling. We have to rely on the <b>Specific words</b> they use, which give us a glimpse into their <b>Model of the world</b>.</p>

<p><b>Language patterns</b> (the words, sentences and structures people use) are <b>Indicators, not the truth</b>, of underlying thought patterns and belief systems. They are among the most powerful tools for helping clients get clarity on their internal and external experiences. Words can <b>Chunk up</b> (specific → abstract) or <b>Chunk down</b> (abstract → specific).</p>

<p><b>Linguistic presuppositions are the assumptions in language</b>: specific language constructs that reveal the speaker's mental assumptions or unique beliefs. Some are trivial and irrelevant; some have <b>Deep, profound implications</b>. If you help a client recognise and change their language, you help them restructure how they see the world, which can be a <b>Catalyst for transformation</b> at both conscious and subconscious levels, because the clues are built into the words they use.</p>

<div class='box key'><div class='box-title'>Key Takeaway</div><p>Step 1 is simply <b>Awareness</b>: learn to notice presuppositions. <b>Awareness precedes change.</b> Step 2 (challenging them) comes in the <b>Clarity Model</b> (Chapter 18). watching this chapter again just to tune your ear to people's language.</p>

</div>

## The presuppositions (NLP training says "about 8 to 10")

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>Type</th><th>What to listen for</th><th>Examples and what to question</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><b>1. Existence</b></td><td><b>Nouns</b>: people (mother, child, teacher), animals (lion, dog), things (table, book, iPad), places (city, coffee shop, zoo), ideas (envy, love, respect, pride)</td><td>"She read a book" presupposes she and the book exist. "<b>We</b> believe..." / "<b>People</b> have said..." — who are "we" and "people"? Mostly trivial, but test the assumption: the story being built may not be grounded in reality.</td></tr><tr><td><b>2. Possibility / necessity</b></td><td>Verbs, adverbs and <b>Modal operators</b> (table below)</td><td>"We <b>Went</b> to the market" (it was possible; the market exists; "we" = more than one) · "You <b>Write</b> neatly in your notebook" · "They <b>Thought</b> about all the prizes"</td></tr><tr><td><b>3. Cause &amp; effect</b></td><td><b>Because, causes, makes, if... then...</b></td><td>"I'm late <b>Because</b> of you" implies you caused it; the two may be unrelated. "<b>If</b> you believe in me <b>Then</b> I can do it": your belief may have nothing to do with their ability. Can be a positive or negative assumption.</td></tr><tr><td><b>4. Complex equivalence</b></td><td><b>Means / means that</b>; the verb "to be" (<b>Is, are</b>). Two things treated as equal: <b>X = Y</b></td><td>"She ignored you again, which <b>Means</b> she's annoyed with you" — she could have been busy. "She told me 'nothing's wrong', which <b>Means</b> something is definitely wrong!" You're making up the meaning.</td></tr><tr><td><b>5. Awareness</b></td><td><b>Hear, see, think, feel</b>: claims to be aware of someone else's experience</td><td>"I could <b>Hear</b> them having that conversation" — how close were you? "I could <b>See</b> she was annoyed" — how did you know? Most of the time we think we know, but we don't.</td></tr><tr><td><b>6. Time</b></td><td>Today, tomorrow, yesterday, often, now, suddenly, after, soon, before, then, immediately, between</td><td>Presupposes that (1) it happened at that time, (2) it took time, (3) you were aware of the time, (4) there was a sequence.</td></tr><tr><td><b>7. Adjective / adverb</b></td><td>Any describing word</td><td>"They live in a <b>Beautiful</b> house" · "Lisa is wearing a <b>Sleeveless</b> shirt today" · "This soup is not <b>Edible</b>" · "He writes <b>Meaningless</b> letters" · "This shop is <b>Much nicer</b>"</td></tr><tr><td><b>8. Inclusive / exclusive "or"</b></td><td>"or", stated or implied</td><td><b>Exclusive</b>: "Chicken or fish?" Two distinct options; choosing one excludes the other. <b>Inclusive</b>: "Coffee on Tuesday or Wednesday?" Both lead to the same outcome (coffee happens). Either way, "or" presupposes only two options exist.</td></tr><tr><td><b>9. Ordinal</b></td><td>First, second, next, last</td><td>"<b>First</b> make the calls, <b>Then</b> we'll talk."</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

### The 6 modal operators

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>Modal operator of...</th><th>Examples</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><b>Necessity</b></td><td>Should, must, ought to, have to</td></tr><tr><td><b>Negative necessity</b></td><td>Shouldn't, mustn't</td></tr><tr><td><b>Probability</b></td><td>Could, may, might, had better</td></tr><tr><td><b>Improbability</b></td><td>Couldn't, may not, might not</td></tr><tr><td><b>Possibility</b></td><td>Able to, can, try, will</td></tr><tr><td><b>Impossibility</b></td><td>Am not, can't, try not, won't</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<p>Why they matter: they set what feels necessary or possible. But <b>Who decides what's possible or impossible?</b> The client is making that meaning. People often move language to the <b>Extremes</b> ("I <i>can't</i>", "I <i>must</i>") when they don't need to. Question: "Why does that <i>have</i> to be the case?"</p>

### Double binds

<p>An <b>Inclusive "or"</b> is what NLP calls a <b>Double bind</b>: it looks like two options, but both lead to one outcome. An exclusive "or" is <b>Not</b> a double bind. You can use these <b>With volition</b> (on purpose) in conversation, e.g. "Would Tuesday or Wednesday work for our next session?"</p>

## Presupposition vs. mind read

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>Presupposition</th><th>Mind read</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>An assumption of the speaker <b>Built into the words they chose</b>. They actually said it (it's inherent in the sentence).</td><td>When the <b>Listener</b> infers or "hallucinates" a meaning that was <b>Not actually stated</b>. You're trying to read the client's mind.</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<p>Both are valuable, but you must tell them apart. <b>Answers</b> to the standard version exercises (P = presupposition, M = mind read):</p>

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>Statement</th><th>Answers </th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>"I don't know why I keep eating chips when I know they're unhealthy for me."</td><td>He eats chips <b>P</b> · He loves the sound of crunching chips <b>M</b> (never mentioned) · He knows chips are unhealthy <b>P</b> · He's obese <b>M</b></td></tr><tr><td>"I want to be better at budgeting my money."</td><td>She has money <b>P</b> (you need money to budget it) · Never taken an accounting class <b>M</b> · Took accounting and failed <b>M</b> · Feels she could improve her budgeting <b>Grey area</b>, leans <b>M</b>: she says she <i>wants</i> to be better, not that she <i>feels</i> she could be</td></tr><tr><td>"I'll never be a millionaire because I don't have any special skills."</td><td>Not now a millionaire <b>P</b> · Believes he has to have special skills to be a millionaire <b>P</b> · Doesn't have / believes he doesn't have any special skills <b>P</b> · Believes he lacks the skills required <b>P</b>. (Even "he" is technically a mind read: the speaker's gender isn't stated.)</td></tr><tr><td>"My manager treats me differently than the other sales people at the office."</td><td>Treats her worse than everyone else <b>M</b> ("differently" isn't negative) · She does sales <b>P</b> ("the <i>other</i> sales people") · Ranks lower in commissions <b>M</b> · Thinks she's treated unfairly <b>M</b></td></tr><tr><td>"You never tell me how you're feeling!"</td><td>Wants to know how she's feeling <b>M</b> (he didn't say he wants to know) · He is frustrated <b>M</b> (tone might suggest it, but he didn't say it) · Wants to leave but doesn't know how <b>M</b> · Believes she has feelings <b>P</b></td></tr></tbody></table></div>

## "What others are really saying": practice

<p><i></i> Suggested answers (more than one may apply):</p>

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>#</th><th>Statement</th><th>Patterns</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>1</td><td>"I can't afford to invest in my new business because I don't have enough clients."</td><td>Impossibility (can't) · cause &amp; effect (because) · comparative (enough) · existence (business)</td></tr><tr><td>2</td><td>"I am hurt because you didn't call me back."</td><td>Cause &amp; effect: you caused my feeling</td></tr><tr><td>3</td><td>"They never tell me when I'm doing a good job, so I can't improve."</td><td>Lack of referential index (they) · universal quantifier (never) · cause &amp; effect (so) · impossibility (can't)</td></tr><tr><td>4</td><td>"You should stop doing that."</td><td>Necessity (should) · lost performative (says who?) · presupposes you're doing it</td></tr><tr><td>5</td><td>"I can tell she likes me by the way she laughs at my jokes."</td><td>Awareness (I can tell) · mind read + complex equivalence (laughing = liking)</td></tr><tr><td>6</td><td>"Either she leaves, or I will."</td><td>Exclusive or (only two options)</td></tr><tr><td>7</td><td>"First you have to make the calls, then we will talk about your raise."</td><td>Ordinal (first, then) · necessity (have to) · time · presupposes a raise conversation</td></tr><tr><td>8</td><td>"Do you want to sign before the bank closes or first thing tomorrow?"</td><td>Inclusive or / double bind (signing is assumed) · time</td></tr><tr><td>9</td><td>"Stop talking about trying to lose weight and just do it now."</td><td>"Trying" presupposes not succeeding · time (now) · presupposes you've been talking about it</td></tr><tr><td>10</td><td>"I really want to change careers but I won't have any security if I paint full-time."</td><td>Cause &amp; effect (if... then) · impossibility (won't) · universal (any) · presupposes wanting to paint</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<div class='box extra'><div class='box-title'>Going deeper</div><p>Presuppositions are why the "I don't know" question works (Chapter 1): "If you <i>did</i> know..." presupposes an answer exists.</p>

<p>Coaches use presuppositions deliberately: "<i>When</i> you reach your goal..." (not "if"), "What will you notice <i>first</i> as things improve?"</p>

<p>Sales people use the inclusive "or" as an <b>Alternative close</b>: "Would Tuesday or Thursday suit you?"</p>

<p>Classic "loaded question": "Have you stopped being late?" Answering yes <i>or</i> no admits you were late. That's the power of presupposition.</p>

<p>Cause &amp; effect and complex equivalence are close cousins: C&amp;E says X <b>Causes</b> Y; complex equivalence says X <b>Means</b> Y.</p>
