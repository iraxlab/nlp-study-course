# 17. Hypnotic Language Patterns (Milton Model)

<p class='subtitle'>Communication Mastery: conversational hypnosis, artfully vague language that expands the client's thinking</p>

<p>This is <b>One of the most challenging videos</b> to get your head around, because it's designed to confuse you. Feeling confused is normal and expected.</p>

## Where it comes from and why it works

<p>These patterns come from <b>Dr Milton Erickson</b>, a famous hypnotherapist with a special skill with language. He used <b>Conversational hypnosis</b>: weaving hypnotic language into an ordinary conversation.</p>

<ul><li><b>Purpose</b>: used <b>With volition</b> (on purpose), it <b>Expands the client's thinking</b> and opens them to new understandings they wouldn't reach through linear, logical thought.</li><li><b>Why confusion helps</b>: when someone is confused, they <b>Latch on to anything they have clarity around</b>. Like during an asthma attack, the only thing you care about is breathing.</li><li><b>The subconscious is a pattern-recogniser.</b> When something doesn't fit its patterns, it pays close attention (being aware helps us survive). Ambiguity uses this.</li><li>The goal <b>Isn't to create clarity</b>, but to create the opportunity for the client to <b>Find clarity themselves</b>.</li><li>The patterns are deliberately <b>Vague</b> (they chunk up), so the listener fills in the meaning from their own experience.</li></ul>

<div class='box key'><div class='box-title'>Key Takeaway</div><p><b>At practitioner level:</b> (1) get <b>Exposure and awareness</b> of the 19 patterns; (2) <b>Listen</b> for them in everyday conversations (people use them without realising); (3) <b>Practise two or three a day</b>, not all 19 at once. Full hypnotic inductions come at <b>Master practitioner</b> level.</p>

</div>

## The 19 patterns

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>#</th><th>Pattern</th><th>What it is</th><th>Examples</th><th>Extra examples / notes</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>1</td><td><b>Mind read</b></td><td>Claiming to know someone's thoughts or feelings without saying how you know</td><td>"I know you are thinking certain things..."</td><td>"I can sense you're feeling certain things now." Creates the illusion you know what they think.</td></tr><tr><td>2</td><td><b>Lost performative</b></td><td>A value judgement where the person making it is deliberately left out</td><td>"And it's a good thing to think those things you're thinking..."</td><td>Who says it's good? There's no reference point.</td></tr><tr><td>3</td><td><b>Cause &amp; effect</b></td><td>Implying one thing causes another: <b>Makes, makes me feel, if... then, as... then, because</b></td><td>"Because it's those thoughts you are thinking that are leading you to new insights..."</td><td>"The fact that you're here makes me feel you're ready for the next level." · "The fact that you're breathing right now is a clear indication you're ready." Shifts the client's focus.</td></tr><tr><td>4</td><td><b>Complex equivalence</b></td><td>Equating two things so their meanings seem the same: <b>Means that</b></td><td>"The fact that you'll be coming to new understandings means that you can take your life in a new and powerful direction."</td><td>Key difference: complex equivalence uses <b>"means that"</b>; cause &amp; effect uses <b>"because" / "if... then"</b>.</td></tr><tr><td>5</td><td><b>Presupposition</b></td><td>The language equivalent of an assumption</td><td>"...and you can feel even more confident of the shifts you're making..."</td><td>Assumes you can feel, that you're confident, and that you're making shifts.</td></tr><tr><td>6</td><td><b>Universal quantifier</b></td><td>Universal, general words with no specific reference (all, every, never)</td><td>"And every shift that you make at the unconscious level now..."</td><td>"Every shift that <i>is</i> made..." is even purer (no "you"). "A whole heap of nothing", but provocative.</td></tr><tr><td>7</td><td><b>Modal operator</b></td><td>Words implying possibility or necessity, which form our rules in life</td><td>"...can allow you to choose to do what you know at some level you really must..."</td><td>Embeds necessity: "...means that you <i>must</i> take this material and learn it."</td></tr><tr><td>8</td><td><b>Nominalisation</b></td><td>A process or action word (verb) frozen into a static noun</td><td>"...deepen your relationship with your unconscious..."</td><td>"Relationship" is the frozen process of relating.</td></tr><tr><td>9</td><td><b>Unspecified verb</b></td><td>No specific action is stated</td><td>"And you can..." · "And you are..."</td><td>You can <i>what</i>? The listener supplies the meaning.</td></tr><tr><td>10</td><td><b>Tag question</b></td><td>A question tagged on the end to lessen resistance</td><td>"...are you not?" · "...haven't you?"</td><td>Not given its own standard version; the practitioner points it out in the script ("and you can, and you are, <b>Are you not?</b>").</td></tr><tr><td>11</td><td><b>Lack of referential index</b></td><td>Doesn't specify who or what it refers to</td><td>"...and one can attain the deepest depths of trance by simply taking a deep breath..."</td><td>"<i>One</i> can", not "you can", but the listener attaches it to themselves.</td></tr><tr><td>12</td><td><b>Comparative deletion</b></td><td>A comparison with no "compared to what or whom"</td><td>"That's the best way to do it." · "It's more or less the right thing to continue drifting as you are now..."</td><td>"This is much better than before." Better than what?</td></tr><tr><td>13</td><td><b>Pacing current experience</b></td><td>Describing the person's verifiable, external experience so the statement is <b>Undeniable</b></td><td>"You're sitting where you're sitting, listening to the sound of my voice and thinking those thoughts that you're thinking."</td><td>Because it's undeniable, the client assumes you had a hand in it. A classic opener: "I remember being in a position much like the one you're in right now..."</td></tr><tr><td>14</td><td><b>Double bind</b></td><td>Two options presented, assuming one or the other will happen (same outcome either way)</td><td>"I don't know if that will cause you to make new connections immediately, or as you just continue to relax..."</td><td>Common in sales. "Dinner Thursday or Friday?" · "Lunchtime today, or 12 noon?" · "Immediately, or as you just blink now?"</td></tr><tr><td>15</td><td><b>Conversational postulate</b></td><td>A yes/no question that avoids being a direct command but prompts action</td><td>"Does that feel like something you can enjoy doing?" · "Can you take another deep breath? And as you do..."</td><td>"Can you shut the door?" / "Can you get me a glass of water?" An <b>Embedded command</b> that looks like a question. Very powerful in rapport.</td></tr><tr><td>16</td><td><b>Extended quote</b></td><td>Quotes nested inside quotes until the listener loses track of perspective; disengages linear, logical thought</td><td>"I'm reminded of a time I had a conversation with a dear friend who told me he had spoken with a child whose father had given him the message that 'you can do, be, have and create anything you desire in life.'"</td><td>Extra favourite: "the <b>Inception</b> tool". The message is four or five times removed, so it slips in when you lose track.</td></tr><tr><td>17</td><td><b>Selectional restriction violation</b></td><td>Giving something qualities it technically or literally can't have</td><td>"And as your chair beckons you to sink even deeper into it..."</td><td>"As your computer screen calls you to focus your attention now." It draws attention and focus.</td></tr><tr><td>18</td><td><b>Ambiguity</b></td><td><b>A) Phonological</b>: sound-alike words · <b>B) Syntactic</b>: a word's function has two meanings · <b>C) Scope</b>: unclear which word is described · <b>D) Punctuation</b>: meaning changes with (missing) punctuation</td><td>A) "You are here and can hear..." · b) "Speaking to you as one who knows..." · c) "How easy it can be to relax and speak when you are in a deep trance..." · d) "I want you to tell me only the things you want to tell me everything you can speak as freely as you like..."</td><td>B) Who is the one who knows: me or you? · c) Used in marketing: "best-selling author and speaker", "number one for customer satisfaction and upload times"</td></tr><tr><td>19</td><td><b>Utilisation</b></td><td>Utilise everything that happens or is said</td><td>"I don't feel like I can change." → "That's right, you don't feel like you can... change consciously... but it's your unconscious where you really change now... isn't it?"</td><td>A door slams in an event: "That's right, when one door closes, another opens, and you're ready to close the door on your past..." Needs <b>Behavioural flexibility</b>.</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<p>The patterns "aren't accurate, none of them": that's the point. They're not statements of fact, they're tools to open someone's thinking. Use them ethically and for the client's benefit.</p>

</div>

<p><b>Utilisation (19)</b> isn't in the written script; it's used live. For example: "And if you're feeling confused, I know you're feeling confused, and that means you're ready to have a powerful transformation..." (which also uses a mind read and complex equivalence).</p>

<div class='box extra'><div class='box-title'>Going deeper</div><p>Source book: <i>Patterns of the Hypnotic Techniques of Milton H. Erickson, M.D.</i> (Bandler &amp; Grinder, 1975).</p>

<p>The Milton Model is the <b>Mirror image of the Meta Model</b> (next chapter). Meta Model = chunk down to get precise. Milton Model = chunk up to be vague. Many patterns appear in both: the Meta Model challenges them, the Milton Model uses them.</p>

<p><b>Embedded commands</b> are closely related: a command hidden inside a longer sentence, often marked by a slight change in tone ("You might find yourself <i>feeling more relaxed now</i>").</p>

<p>Everyday uses: coaching, presentations, parenting ("Do you want to brush your teeth before or after your story?" is a double bind), and speeches. Use them ethically, for the client's benefit.</p>

<p>Easy patterns to start practising: <b>Pacing current experience</b>, <b>Double binds</b>, <b>Conversational postulates</b> and <b>Tag questions</b>. They fit naturally into ordinary conversation.</p>
