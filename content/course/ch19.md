# 19. Reframing: Meaning & Context Reframes

<p class='subtitle'>Communication Mastery: helping someone see the same thing from a new angle</p>

<p>Often you don't have 90 minutes or three hours for a major life intervention; you might only have <b>A few moments</b> to help someone see the world differently. Reframes are <b>Conversational tools</b> for exactly that.</p>

## Why reframing works

<ul><li><b>Awareness and clarity precede change.</b> A person needs to see another opportunity or point of view, and be clear on how they're seeing things now.</li><li>Our <b>Model of the world is as unique as a thumbprint</b>. We believe things are true simply because we believe them.</li><li><b>We judge others by their actions, and ourselves by our intentions.</b> ("That wasn't my intention." Well, that's how it came across, so own it.)</li><li>Reframes use <b>Questions</b> to show there are more angles and perspectives than the client was holding on to. <b>That perspective shift can do 80% of the heavy lifting.</b></li><li>In the perspective shift, <b>Emotions settle and reasoning and understanding come in</b>.</li></ul>

</div>

## The two reframes

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th></th><th><b>Meaning reframe</b></th><th><b>Context reframe</b></th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>What it does</td><td>Gives an <b>Alternative meaning</b> to the same event or behaviour; the client sees it from another perspective</td><td>Takes the <b>Same behaviour</b> and moves it into a <b>New context</b> where it becomes positive</td></tr><tr><td>Difficulty</td><td>Simple</td><td>More complex: needs objective thinking</td></tr><tr><td>Ask yourself</td><td>"<b>What else could this behaviour mean?</b>"</td><td>"<b>What's another context where the same thing would take on a very different meaning?</b>"</td></tr><tr><td>How it works</td><td>Stay in the same situation; step into the <b>Other person's shoes</b></td><td>Separate the behaviour from its context; <b>Remove the characters</b> and place the behaviour somewhere new (real or made-up)</td></tr><tr><td>Best for</td><td>Any negative interpretation</td><td>Judgement statements: "<b>I'm too...</b>", "<b>She's too...</b>", "<b>He's too...</b>"</td></tr><tr><td>Typical opener</td><td>"<b>Isn't it wonderful that...</b>" (the practitioner's usual start)</td><td>"Isn't it wonderful that <b>When</b> [new situation], she'll be able to..."</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<div class='box key'><div class='box-title'>Key Takeaway</div><p><b>If you change the context, you automatically change the meaning. If you change the meaning, you don't necessarily change the context.</b></p>

<p>Nothing has meaning except the meaning we give it. People don't realise they're <i>choosing</i> meaning; they treat their interpretation as fact and build more conclusions on it.</p>

</div>

## 1. Meaning reframe

<p>People assign meaning all the time without knowing they're doing it. Their filters shape the interpretation, they treat it as true, right or wrong, and then build further experiences on that first, possibly ill-conceived idea. A meaning reframe offers an <b>Alternative meaning</b> (and there aren't one or two, there are dozens). It also challenges the <b>Presuppositions, complex equivalences and cause &amp; effect</b> built into what they said (Chapters 16 and 18).</p>

### Useful questions

<ul><li>"What else could this behaviour or event mean?"</li><li>"Is it possible you haven't noticed another perspective?"</li><li>"Isn't it possible that it could mean something different?"</li><li>"Is it possible that what you believe it means may not actually be true? Could it mean something else entirely?"</li></ul>

### Examples

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>Client says (negative)</th><th>Meaning reframe (positive)</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>"He's too controlling."</td><td>Maybe he wants to give you extra support.</td></tr><tr><td>"Mum's always checking up on me. I'm being smothered."</td><td>"Isn't it lovely that she cares about you?" Maybe she's just trying to show you she loves you.</td></tr><tr><td>"She's too much of a perfectionist around the house."</td><td>"Isn't it wonderful that she cares so much about things being a certain way?" Maybe she has high standards and wants a beautiful home for you.</td></tr><tr><td>"She doesn't do anything around the house."</td><td>"Isn't it wonderful that she's spending time on the things she really cares about and loves to do?"</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

## 2. Context reframe

<p>Take the dynamic, remove the characters, and plant the same behaviour in a new environment where it's a <b>Strength</b>.</p>

### Case study: the client and his "stubborn" daughter

<p>The client storms into a coach's office: <b>"You need to fix her. She's too stubborn, she doesn't listen to me."</b> His 16-year-old daughter won't follow the university and career path he wants. (Underneath: he's over-caring about being a good parent.)</p>

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>New context</th><th>Context reframe the practitioner used or suggested</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><b>A house party</b> (the one he used)</td><td>"Isn't it wonderful that when she's at a party this weekend, has had a few drinks, and some guy tries to take advantage of her, she'll be strong enough and stubborn enough to say <b>No</b>, keep healthy boundaries, and not fall victim to other people's wishes?" the client was furious and proud at the same time, then calmed down: "Yeah, that's right."</td></tr><tr><td><b>Future relationship</b></td><td>"Isn't it wonderful that when she's in a relationship, she'll be strong enough to set healthy boundaries with people who may not have her best interests at heart?"</td></tr><tr><td><b>Work</b></td><td>If a boss pushes her to stay late or work weekends, she has the boundaries to keep her work and home time separate.</td></tr><tr><td><b>Rejection</b></td><td>"Isn't it wonderful that when she gets knocked back on one of her dreams, she'll be stubborn enough to ignore the criticism and find another way? You've got a daughter who believes in herself."</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

### Meaning reframes for the same situation

<p>The practitioner could also stay in the same context (father and daughter) and reframe the meaning:</p>

<ul><li><b>Simple version:</b> "Isn't it wonderful that she's so strong and confident in her own skin that she'll stand up for what she believes in? You don't want a pushover; surely you want her to think for herself."</li><li><b>The "you taught her" version:</b> the practitioner first gets the client to agree he's the one responsible for raising her, and that he's a strong-willed man who backs himself. Then: "Well done! You've passed those strengths on to her. Isn't it also possible you could pass on wisdom, experience and mentorship too?" the client moves from "I'm right, she's wrong" to "she learned this from me, so I can teach her more."</li></ul>

<div class='box extra'><div class='box-title'>Going deeper</div><p><b>Classic context reframes in stories:</b> the Ugly Duckling (awkward as a duckling, beautiful as a swan) and Rudolph's red nose (mocked by the other reindeer, a hero in the fog). Same trait, new context, new meaning.</p>

<p>The source book is <i>Reframing</i> (Bandler &amp; Grinder, 1982). NLP's related presupposition: <b>Every behaviour has a positive intention</b>; a reframe often uncovers it.</p>

<p>Advanced versions: <b>Six-Step Reframing</b> (working with the "part" responsible for a behaviour) and Robert Dilts' <b>Sleight of Mouth</b> patterns (14 ways to reframe a belief).</p>

<p>Psychology research backs this up: <b>Cognitive reappraisal</b> (changing how you interpret a situation) reliably reduces negative emotion and is linked with better wellbeing (James Gross, Stanford).</p>

<p>"We judge ourselves by our intentions and others by their behaviour" is a line usually credited to Stephen M. R. Covey (<i>The Speed of Trust</i>). In psychology this bias is called the <b>Actor–observer bias</b>.</p>

<p>Reframing isn't telling someone their feelings are wrong. Keep <b>Rapport</b>, acknowledge how they feel, then offer the new angle as a question ("Isn't it possible...?").</p>

</div>
