# 20. Emotional Mastery: Overview

<p class='subtitle'>Emotional Mastery: understanding emotions so you can master them, not just manage them</p>

<p>The practitioner calls emotional mastery <b>Some of the most powerful material in NLP training</b> and something he uses every day. The tools are simple and accessible, yet they're also used at the <b>Highest levels of sport</b>.</p>

## Your emotions shape your quality of life

<ul><li>Even retelling the story brings those feelings back. Recalling a memory <b>Reinforces</b> its emotional pattern.</li><li>The same is true for negative memories: <b>Keep replaying the times you messed up and you strengthen that pattern too.</b></li><li>Learning to <b>Flex the memory</b> of good states is one of the most powerful things you can do.</li></ul>

## Key principles

### 1. There are no positive or negative emotions

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>Instead of...</th><th>Think...</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Positive / negative</td><td><b>Effective / ineffective</b> · <b>Supportive / limiting</b> · <b>Working for you / working against you</b></td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<ul><li><b>Anger or frustration</b> can be a powerful tool when used for something positive. Are you using the emotion, or is it using you?</li><li><b>Relaxed and blissful</b> won't help in a crisis, where you need to be calm but also decisive, certain and determined.</li><li><b>Energised and determined</b> won't help when you're trying to meditate.</li><li><b>Every emotion is serving you</b>: as a reminder, a trigger to become aware, a wake-up call to shift, or a sign you're choosing to indulge a pattern. If it's not working for you, it's working for you <b>As a reminder to change</b>.</li></ul>

### 2. Nothing has meaning except the meaning we give it

<p>Every experience is a <b>Blank canvas</b>; we colour it in with meaning. Examples: a client found the word "babe" disrespectful, while to others it's affectionate. In a well-known interview, Oprah Winfrey and Jay-Z disagreed about a racial slur used in rap: to Oprah it carried a painful history; Jay-Z said that for his community it had been taken back and used differently. Same word, different meaning, so they agreed to disagree.</p>

<p><b>Circumstance + emotion = memory.</b> That's why you remember where you were on 9/11, your wedding day, your children's births and your first breakup. These <b>Significant Emotional Events</b> can be tapped for resources (Chapter 5).</p>

### 3. Emotional mastery, not emotional management

<p>Most people only <i>manage</i> emotions. No school teaches how emotions are created, how to understand them or how to use them, so people often make poor decisions about what their emotions mean.</p>

### 4. Emotions are indicators

<p>Like a car's <b>Fuel light</b>: it doesn't mean the car is broken, just that it's time to refuel. An emotion is a warning light that something is either aligned (you're living your purpose) or out of alignment (friction with your values, beliefs, behaviour patterns or standards). It <b>Indicates</b>; it doesn't diagnose.</p>

### 5. Emotions are transient

<p>They pass <b>Like clouds on a breezy afternoon</b>. Even people with clinical depression or anxiety move in and out of different emotions during the day; the skill is learning to label states and shift more quickly.</p>

<p><b>The clicker metaphor:</b> a 100–200 g remote is easy to hold out at arm's length, but not for an hour. Its weight never changes; <b>Holding on</b> is what hurts. Feeling an emotion isn't exhausting; <b>Holding on to it is</b>. Many people also suffer from <b>Judging themselves</b> for feeling it.</p>

<div class='box key'><div class='box-title'>Key Takeaway</div><p>"Nothing, and I repeat nothing, has meaning except the meaning we give to it."</p>

<p>No positive or negative emotions, only <b>Effective or ineffective</b>. Every emotion is an <b>Indicator</b> and is <b>Transient</b>. Holding on is the problem, not feeling.</p>

</div>

## The "12 emotions" idea and awareness

<p>The average adult experiences about <b>12 emotions a day: 8 negative, 4 positive</b>, and for most people it's <b>The same 12 every day</b>. So your future becomes a "well-rehearsed past". You can upgrade your house, car and circumstances, but if those 12 emotions don't change, you'll be the same person: "same stuff, different day". <b>Awareness precedes change.</b></p>

## Emotions and triggers

<p>Every emotion usually has a <b>Conscious or subconscious trigger</b> (from <b>Ivan Pavlov</b>'s work on conditioning). Emotions and habits are "family members" in the nervous system: emotions trigger behaviours and behaviours trigger emotions.</p>

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>Trigger</th><th>Emotion it creates (Extra examples)</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>The smell and first sip of the morning coffee with the client</td><td>Calm, energy, excitement for the day</td></tr><tr><td>A partner's look, a snap, or "We need to talk"</td><td>Knowing you're in trouble</td></tr><tr><td>A child's cry</td><td>Parents can tell a real-injury cry from a sibling-squabble cry</td></tr><tr><td>Coach feedback that challenged his integrity</td><td>Frustration and anger</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<p>Some triggers create more than one emotion, and some emotions have more than one trigger.</p>

### Become the observer: dissociation

<p>Describe your emotion in the <b>Third person</b>: "the practitioner was feeling excited. What was going on around the practitioner that triggered it?" This makes you the <b>Observer</b> of your emotional experience. (Double dissociation, watching yourself watching yourself, is a more advanced tool.)</p>

### Two levels of change

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>Level</th><th>What you do</th><th>Tools (coming later)</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><b>1. The trigger</b></td><td>Dissolve the trigger, so the emotion never shows up</td><td>Strategy scrambler, resource anchors, collapsing anchors, chain of anchors, momentum pattern, reframes, metaphors</td></tr><tr><td><b>2. The emotion</b></td><td>When the trigger fires, respond positively instead of negatively</td><td>Collapsing anchor, momentum pattern</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<p><b>Case study, the client:</b> a university student who snapped at her mum every time she came home and was asked about her day. She felt her mum was prying; her mum just loved her. Using a momentum pattern, the practitioner collapsed the negative anchor and installed positive ones. Weeks later her mum found the practitioner at an event: "I wanted to meet the man who helped me reconnect with my daughter. We've never had a better relationship."</p>

<div class='box extra'><div class='box-title'>Going deeper</div><p><b>Name it to tame it</b>: brain-imaging research (Lieberman et al., 2007) found that putting feelings into words ("affect labelling") reduces activity in the amygdala, the brain's alarm system. The practitioner's hourly emotion log uses exactly this effect.</p>

<p><b>Emotional granularity</b> (Lisa Feldman Barrett): people who can name emotions precisely ("disappointed" or "resentful" instead of just "bad") tend to regulate them better. Try an emotion wheel, such as <b>Plutchik's wheel of emotions</b>, to widen your vocabulary.</p>

<p>Duhigg's <b>Habit loop</b> is <b>Cue (trigger) → routine → reward</b>; the standard teaching use the same model in the Behavioural Change section.</p>

<p><b>Pavlov</b> (Nobel Prize 1904) found that dogs began to salivate at a sound that had been paired with food: <b>Classical conditioning</b>, the science behind NLP anchoring.</p>

<p>Psychologist <b>James Gross</b>' research on emotion regulation shows that strategies used early (choosing situations, changing attention, reappraisal) work better than suppressing emotions after they arrive, which matches the practitioner's idea of working at the trigger level.</p>
