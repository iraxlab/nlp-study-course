## Step 1: Your 5 power emotions

<p>Write down <b>Five emotions you'd love to feel every day</b>: ones that, if you could click your fingers and step into them, would <b>Uplift you, inspire you and let you do your best work</b>. Some you already feel and just want to <b>Magnify</b>; others may feel like "a bridge too far". Both are fine; you'll build on them with resource anchors and momentum patterns. (Use Worksheet C in Chapter 20.)</p>

<p>Optional: list some positive emotions and their triggers too, the same way you did for the negative ones. Knowing what makes you feel good is just as valuable.</p>

## Where anchoring comes from: Pavlov

<p><b>Ivan Pavlov</b> discovered <b>Stimulus → response</b>. His dogs salivated when food arrived. He started ringing a bell before every feed (bell, food; bell, food...). Eventually he rang the bell <b>Without</b> food, and the dogs <b>Still salivated</b>. A trigger with no real connection to food now produced the response. (the practitioner's joke: the name might "ring a bell".)</p>

<blockquote class='quote'>"The secret to emotional mastery is emotional conditioning."</blockquote>

<ul><li><b>Emotional conditioning</b> = conditioning yourself to feel good even when you don't want to, by training your body and nervous system.</li><li>Most people have <b>Negative</b> emotional conditioning: they keep repeating and reinforcing negative emotions, then wonder why they struggle with self-doubt, self-worth and confidence. If you've doubted yourself your whole life, you've built that muscle. <b>You've been ringing that bell for a long time.</b></li><li><b>You get in life whatever you practise.</b> the practitioner: "I don't have self-doubt issues, but if I practised doubting myself every day for 30 days, I probably would." He practises confidence, certainty and decisiveness daily by challenging himself to be them.</li></ul>

<blockquote class='quote'>"Don't fake it till you make it. Work on it until you own it."</blockquote>

<blockquote class='quote'>"Emotions are skills."</blockquote>

<p>Your five power emotions are <b>Skills</b>. Everyone is motivated about something (things aligned with their values), and if you practise motivation, you become a motivated person. The practitioner applies the same idea to low moods: rehearsing and reinforcing an emotion strengthens it, and he says he isn't trying to diminish how serious depression is. <b>It all comes down to stimulus and response.</b></p>

<p>Step 1 is <b>Awareness</b> of your anchors; step 2 is using them <b>With volition</b> (on purpose).</p>

## The types of anchor

<p>The practitioner covers <b>Six</b>: one for each sense plus self-talk.</p>

"Cuddle my head" when he's sick; hugging people at events · <b>Clothes</b>: a bikini, lingerie or heels can trigger confidence or insecurity; a suit changes how a man holds himself · The <b>All Blacks' haka</b> (below)</td></tr><tr><td><b>Auditory</b> (sound)</td><td>Songs, jingles, sounds, voices</td><td>Feeling sad whenever a certain song plays, long after forgetting why · coins rattling bringing back your father</td><td>Jingles and slogans: McDonald's, Nike's "Just do it" · Distinctive voices: Morgan Freeman, Sean Connery, Barack Obama</td></tr><tr><td><b>Visual</b> (sight)</td><td>Images, logos, people, looks</td><td>Seeing a colleague and feeling happy (+) or angry (−) · a traffic light turning green and pressing the accelerator</td><td>Logos · Sporting moments (Michael Jordan's final shot) · Seeing a long-lost friend · A partner's "look" · Traffic lights (a universal anchor)</td></tr><tr><td><b>Olfactory</b> (smell)</td><td>Scents that bring back states and memories</td><td>Apple pie taking you back to Mum's kitchen · the smell of a fast-food restaurant bringing back anger from an argument there</td><td>Incense, essential oils · Smelling salts · Anchovies</td></tr><tr><td><b>Gustatory</b> (taste)</td><td>Tastes and foods tied to states</td><td>Feeling ill at a food that once gave you food poisoning · the first taste of chocolate bringing relief</td><td>Smoking · The <b>Toothpaste tingle</b>: when a tingling ingredient was removed, sales dropped; ### The All Blacks' haka

<p>NLP didn't take this from the All Blacks, but it identifies the same tool: a strong <b>Kinaesthetic anchor</b>.<b>"power move" or "signature move"</b>. In NLP you'll learn <b>Resource anchors</b>, <b>Collapsing anchors</b> and the <b>Neurological bridge</b>, which are kinaesthetic anchors "without the song and dance".</p>

## Designing anchors into your life

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>Example anchors at home</th><th>Type</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>A rusty sign at the front door: "Only those who dare to fail greatly can ever achieve greatly." He reads it as he walks in, as a reminder to stretch himself and be bold</td><td>Visual + auditory digital</td></tr><tr><td>Photos around the house that bring back memories and experiences</td><td>Visual</td></tr><tr><td>Mood playlists: high energy, love songs, relaxing, background</td><td>Auditory</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<p>To design your own, ask three questions:</p>

<ol><li><b>What do I want to feel?</b></li><li><b>What trigger or memory can I link to that feeling?</b></li><li><b>What can be the vehicle or conduit</b> (a song, photo, scent, object, phrase)?</li></ol>

<p>And remember: <b>None of this has meaning except the meaning we give to it</b>.</p>

## The 5 keys to a strong anchor

<p>There are five keys: <b>Intensity, Timing, Uniqueness, Replicable, Number of times</b>. them as the acronym <b>ITURN</b> in the next chapter (Chapter 22).</p>

</div>

<div class='box extra'><div class='box-title'>Going deeper</div><p><b>Smell and memory</b>: smells travel almost directly to the brain's emotional and memory centres (the amygdala and hippocampus), which is why odour-evoked memories feel especially vivid and emotional (Rachel Herz's research). This is sometimes called the <b>Proust effect</b>.</p>

<p><b>Enclothed cognition</b> (Adam &amp; Galinsky, 2012): people wearing what they were told was a doctor's lab coat paid more attention in tests than those told it was a painter's coat. Clothes really can change how we think and act, as in Extra example.</p>

<p><b>Pre-performance routines</b> in sport (a tennis player bouncing the ball the same number of times, a goal-kicker's set-up) are anchors. Research reviews link consistent routines with better focus and performance under pressure.</p>

<p><b>Music and mood</b>: listening to music you love releases dopamine in the brain's reward system (Salimpoor et al., 2011), which is why mood playlists work.</p>

<p>The <i>5 Love Languages</i> is by the client Chapman (1992): words of affirmation, quality time, receiving gifts, acts of service and physical touch.</p>
