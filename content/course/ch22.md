# 22. State Elicitation (and ITURN)

<p class='subtitle'>Emotional Mastery: recalling a state on demand, the building block of every anchor</p>

<p>This chapter has two parts: the <b>ITURN</b> rules for creating powerful anchors, then the <b>State elicitation script</b> with a live worked example.</p>

<p><b>What an anchor is:</b> the ability to <b>Access a state on demand</b>.</p>

## Part 1: ITURN, the 5 keys to a powerful anchor

<blockquote class='quote'>I · T · U · R · N</blockquote>

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>Letter</th><th>Key</th><th>What the practitioner teaches</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><b>I</b></td><td><b>Intensity</b></td><td>The more intense the emotion, the easier it is to remember and recall. That's why you remember milestones: birthdays, wins, your first kiss, weddings, divorces, the birth of your children, world tragedies.</td></tr><tr><td><b>T</b></td><td><b>Timing</b></td><td>Every emotion has an <b>Arc</b>: it builds, peaks, then drops off. Anchor at the <b>Peak</b>, the top <b>10–20%</b> of the emotional range: build it up, trigger, release. Not at the start or the end.</td></tr><tr><td><b>U</b></td><td><b>Uniqueness</b></td><td>The anchor must be unique so it isn't fired <b>By accident</b>; done on purpose (with volition) and under conscious control. But not so unique that you can't repeat it.</td></tr><tr><td><b>R</b></td><td><b>Replicability</b></td><td>Done <b>Exactly the same way</b> every time. The practitioner's power move: he visualises <b>Breaking through a pane of glass and stepping into his power</b>, done the same way for about a decade, so doing it now takes him straight back.</td></tr><tr><td><b>N</b></td><td><b>Number of times</b></td><td>More repetitions = a stronger anchor. But it's the <b>Least important</b>: get the other four right first, every time.</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

### Three ways to access a state

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>Source</th><th>What it is</th><th>Notes</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><b>1. Naturally occurring</b></td><td>Anchor it in the moment, while it's happening (you've just won, something amazing has happened)</td><td>The most powerful, but the hardest to replicate easily and authentically. If you're in it, anchor it.</td></tr><tr><td><b>2. Recalled (remembered)</b></td><td>Remember being there and fully saturate your nervous system in the emotion</td><td>This is <b>State elicitation</b>: the main method here.</td></tr><tr><td><b>3. Created / projected / imagined</b></td><td>Imagine the state</td><td>For clients who say they've <b>Never</b> felt it.</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<p><b>"I've never felt confident."</b> This is called out: "Are you confident about that? Then you're confident." Are you confident you can tie your shoes, drive a car, breathe? "I'm not confident, I'm <i>certain</i>." Great: <b>Certainty is an extension of confidence</b>. To know you don't have something, you must have a reference for it, so you've felt it before and can remember it.</p>


<div class='box key'><div class='box-title'>Key Takeaway</div><p><b>ITURN is the foundation for creating resource anchors.</b> Intensity, Timing (at the peak), Uniqueness, Replicability, Number of times. Number of times matters least.</p>

</div>

## Part 2: The state elicitation script

<p>State elicitation simply means <b>Remembering or recalling an emotional experience</b> so the person feels it again. Read the script; as <b>"use the tools, don't be a tool."</b></p>

<div class='script'><div class='script-row'><b>1.</b> "Can you recall a time when you were totally <b>[state]</b>?"</div><div class='script-row'><b>2.</b> "Can you remember a <b>Specific</b> time?"</div><div class='script-row'><b>3.</b> "As you go back to that time now, go right back to that time, <b>Float down into your body</b> and <b>See what you saw</b>, <b>Hear what you heard</b>, and <b>Really feel the feelings</b> of being totally <b>[state]</b>."</div></div>

<ul><li><b>Why "specific"?</b> One specific moment is easier for the brain to fully saturate.</li><li>Any state works: totally excited, in love, happy, or angry. Positive and negative.</li><li>The person should be <b>Fully associated</b>: first person, looking through their own eyes.</li></ul>

### What to watch for (sensory acuity, Chapter 9)

<p>Facial expression and body language · a shift in <b>Breathing</b> · <b>Skin colour and tone</b> (flushed or light) · <b>Pupil</b> dilation · <b>Lower lip</b> size · <b>Heart rate</b> (racing or calm). Even with eyes closed you can see the changes. That's how you know the client is getting into state.</p>

### Doing it on yourself

<ul><li>Pick an emotion and practise remembering and reliving it. You don't need to ask yourself the questions out loud.</li><li>Some people find it easy, others struggle with details. That's fine. People recall differently: about 80% of incoming information is visual, but you might remember sounds best (auditory) or feelings best (kinaesthetic). Very visual people can often "replay" an experience in detail.</li><li><b>It's a trained skill</b>, like meditation: people who practise get good at it. Don't beat yourself up.</li></ul>

## Worked example

<p>Between memories the practitioner had the client <b>Open and close her eyes</b>, a quick break state before the next one. Asking about colour, location, loud or soft goes beyond the basic script; these are <b>Submodalities</b>, covered later. <b>The foundation is just: what it looks like, sounds like and feels like.</b></p>

<div class='box extra'><div class='box-title'>Going deeper</div><p>Psychologists use the same method in research: the <b>Autobiographical recall</b> technique ("write about a time you felt...") is a standard, reliable way to induce emotions in lab studies.</p>

<p>Sports psychology uses <b>Imagery</b> the same way. The <b>PETTLEP</b> model (Holmes &amp; Collins, 2001) says mental rehearsal works best when it's vivid, first-person and includes physical feelings, just like "float down into your body".</p>

<p>Recalling a memory also <b>Updates</b> it (memory reconsolidation), one reason why reliving good memories on purpose can strengthen them.</p>

<p><b>Break state</b>: briefly distracting the person (open your eyes, "what did you have for breakfast?") clears one state before eliciting the next. You'll use this in collapse anchors.</p>
