# 23. Precision Anchoring

<p class='subtitle'>Emotional Mastery: locking a positive state onto a knuckle</p>

<p>State elicitation gets someone to <b>Feel</b> a state. It doesn't <b>Anchor</b> it. A <b>Precision anchor</b> uses a touch point (kinaesthetic receptors) to lock that state in, so it can be fired on demand.</p>

## The boat metaphor

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>Beautiful day: blue skies, clear water</th><th>Terrible day: rain, wind, huge seas</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>You <b>Drop anchor</b>: it locks you in place so you can enjoy it longer</td><td>You <b>Don't</b> drop anchor: you want to get out of there as fast as possible</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<p>Most people do the opposite: they anchor <b>Negative</b> emotions and never anchor positive ones. Feeling bad is easy and feeling good isn't, because they haven't trained it. If you don't train yourself, <b>The media, the school system and others will train you</b>. The subconscious also leans towards <b>Risk over reward</b> for safety.</p>

<p><b>Why the knuckle?</b> It's obvious whether you're touching it or not, so it's easy to <b>Replicate exactly</b>. A shoulder or hand is hard to hit in the same spot every time (unless there's a freckle or tattoo). The knuckle is universal and convenient, but any spot works as long as it follows <b>ITURN</b>.</p>

## The 5 steps

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>Step</th><th>What to do</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><b>1. Identify</b></td><td>Choose the positive state to anchor. <b>One at a time.</b></td></tr><tr><td><b>2. Elicit</b></td><td>Use the state elicitation script (Chapter 22): "Can you remember a time when you felt [state]? A specific time? Go back to that time now; notice what it looks like, sounds like and feels like..."</td></tr><tr><td><b>3. Anchor on the knuckle</b></td><td>Build the state up with the counting ladder (below) and press the knuckle <b>Only at the peak</b>.</td></tr><tr><td><b>4. Repeat 5 times</b></td><td>Repeat, ideally with a <b>Different memory</b> each time, on the <b>Same knuckle</b>, with a break state in between.</td></tr><tr><td><b>5. Test</b></td><td>Break state, then press the knuckle and notice what comes up.</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

### The counting ladder (getting intensity and timing right)

<div class='script'><div class='script-row'>"Now let's build that up, with every number <b>Doubling</b> the feeling of [state]: from five to six... six to seven... seven to eight... eight to nine... <b>[press]</b> nine to ten... now <b>Double</b> that feeling... take a deep breath... and <b>Release</b> <b>[let go]</b>."</div></div>

<ul><li>The ladder builds <b>Intensity</b> and makes the <b>Timing</b> easy: you only touch the knuckle from <b>9 → 10 → double it → release</b>, the very top of the emotional range.</li><li><b>Common mistake:</b> touching the knuckle the whole time (from 5 up to 10 and back down). The anchor then picks up the low start and low finish, so the test feels weak.</li><li><b>Build it up, anchor at the top, release.</b></li></ul>

<div class='box key'><div class='box-title'>Key Takeaway</div><p>For now, <b>Only anchor positive emotions</b>. Negative ones are used later in collapse anchors.</p>

<p><b>Remember which positive state and knuckle you used</b>: you'll use them to collapse a negative state.</p>

</div>

## Worked example

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>Stage</th><th>What happened</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Set-up</td><td>Client sits close beside you, <b>Fists resting on their knees</b>. Roll up sleeves and watch for rings. Use the <b>Index knuckle of the hand closest to you</b>.</td></tr><tr><td>State chosen</td><td><b>Passionate</b> (the practitioner didn't know beforehand)</td></tr><tr><td>Anchoring</td><td>Three rounds, each with a <b>Different memory</b> of feeling passionate: script → ladder 5 to 10 → double it → press at the peak → deep breath → release. <b>"Open your eyes, close your eyes"</b> between rounds as a quick reset.</td></tr><tr><td>Break state</td><td>"Shake your body out. What did you have for breakfast?" (<i>A smoothie.</i>)</td></tr><tr><td>Test</td><td>Eyes closed, hands back in position, knuckle pressed, deep breath. "What memories, images, sounds or feelings come up?" the client: recent work for clients, raising money for the bushfires in January, workouts in the garage.</td></tr><tr><td>After</td><td>"How are you feeling?" "Good... passionate."</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

### What to look for when testing

<ul><li>You're <b>Not</b> looking for the client to say anything specific; use <b>Sensory acuity</b>.</li><li>Watch for colour changes and facial shifts, and with strong positive states, changes in <b>Posture, stance and breathing</b>. Don't be surprised if they take a big breath on the test: you've been anchoring that breath in.</li><li>For a real client, this will usually be a <b>Brand-new experience</b>, so watch closely.</li></ul>

<div class='box extra'><div class='box-title'>Going deeper</div><p><b>Negativity bias</b> is well documented: "Bad is stronger than good" (Baumeister et al., 2001) reviewed hundreds of studies showing negative events affect us more than positive ones of the same size. That's why positive states need deliberate practice.</p>

<p>Rick Hanson's <b>"taking in the good"</b> (<i>Hardwiring Happiness</i>): staying with a positive experience for 10–20 seconds helps it stick, which is the same idea as anchoring at the peak.</p>

<p>Sport psychologists call these <b>Cue words</b> and <b>Trigger routines</b> (Hewitt's "C'mon!" is a classic example).</p>
