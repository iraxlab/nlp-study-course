# 24. Neural Bridge (Chain of Anchors)

<p class='subtitle'>Emotional Mastery: moving from a stuck state to a resourceful one, step by step</p>

<p>This is called <b>One of the most complex and most powerful</b> tools in Emotional Mastery; everything so far builds towards it. A neural (neurological) bridge is <b>A series of collapsed anchors linked together</b>, three or four in a row.</p>

</div>

<div class='box fact'><div class='box-title'>Fact check</div><ul><li>If anything here is unclear, read Chapter 25 first; a short primer is below.</li></ul></div>

### Quick primer: what's a collapse anchor?

<p>A negative state is anchored on one knuckle and a stronger positive state on another. Both are fired together; the negative is released first and the positive is held and built up. The positive "collapses" the negative, so the old trigger loses its charge. (Full details in Chapter 25.)</p>

## When to use it

<p>For <b>Stuck</b> emotional states: <b>Hesitation, procrastination, lethargy, self-sabotage</b>, when someone feels they just can't move forward. Also: fear → confidence, anger → playfulness, frustration → calm, stuck with no options → excited by possibilities.</p>

<p><b>Why not just one collapse anchor?</b> Going straight from procrastination to motivation is <b>"a bridge too far"</b>, too big a jump. So you put <b>Baby steps</b> in between: a bridge the person can walk across emotionally.</p>

<blockquote class='quote'>Procrastination → Frustration → Determination → Excitement → Motivation</blockquote>

<p>There should be a <b>Natural arc</b>, "like a plane taking off". Eventually, the old stuck state <b>Automatically</b> triggers the chain and leads to the final state, so neither the original anchor nor the chain needs to be fired again .</p>

## Bridge design: the rules

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>Rule</th><th>Explanation</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><b>Decide the first and last state</b></td><td>Where the client is now and where they want to be. These two matter most.</td></tr><tr><td><b>4–5 states in total</b></td><td>Minimum 4, maximum 5. Negotiate the 2–3 states in between with the client.</td></tr><tr><td><b>State 2 must be a negative, "away-from" state</b></td><td>E.g. <b>Frustration</b>: it has energy. People will do more to avoid pain than to gain pleasure, so use that on purpose ("sick and tired of being sick and tired").</td></tr><tr><td><b>States 3, 4, 5 must be positive, "towards" states</b></td><td>E.g. determination, excitement, focus, motivation</td></tr><tr><td><b>The second-to-last state is a towards state</b></td><td>The state just before the final one pulls the person forward, so the last step feels natural.</td></tr><tr><td><b>Sufficiently intense</b></td><td>Ask: "Is this state strong enough to carry you on to the next one?" If not, pick a stronger word or add a modifier.</td></tr><tr><td><b>All states must have movement</b></td><td>Each one should be energising enough to carry the chain on to the next .</td></tr><tr><td><b>Each state bigger than the last</b></td><td>Build, build, build to a crescendo. Don't drop into a calm state like peace halfway through.</td></tr><tr><td><b>Avoid major negative emotions</b></td><td>Use mild ones (frustration, irritation, annoyance), not anger, hatred, disgust or shame.</td></tr><tr><td><b>Self-initiated</b></td><td>The client chooses the states; don't tell them what to feel.</td></tr><tr><td><b>Not how they already do it</b></td><td>Their current strategy isn't working; design a new one.</td></tr><tr><td><b>A natural, obvious progression</b></td><td>Each step should feel like "Oh yeah, that's what I'd feel next."</td></tr><tr><td><b>Use modifiers</b> </td><td>Words that tune the strength of a state, e.g. "a little frustrated", "really determined", "completely motivated". <i></i></td></tr></tbody></table></div>

## The steps

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>Step</th><th>What to do</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><b>1. Decide the states</b></td><td>Map out 4–5 states. Check the chain with the client: eyes closed, walk through it ("If you got frustrated, would that give you energy? If you then became determined...?"). Ask: <b>"Any weak links?"</b></td></tr><tr><td><b>2. Anchor each state</b></td><td>One knuckle per state, <b>Pinky → thumb</b> (start with the knuckle closest to you). <b>State 1</b> (the stuck one): anchor <b>Once</b>, no build-up. <b>States 2–5</b>: precision-anchor each with the 5 → 10 ladder, "<b>Stacked</b>" (5 times each). <b>Break state between every one</b> so there's no emotional bleed. "Minimise the bad guy, maximise the good guy."</td></tr><tr><td><b>3. Chain them (the bridge)</b></td><td>Fire 1 → add 2 → release 1 and double down on 2 → add 3 → release 2 → add 4 → release 3 → add 5 → release 4 → <b>Hold 5 and build it all the way up</b> (8, 9, 10, double, triple) → deep breath → release.</td></tr><tr><td><b>4. Test</b></td><td>Break state, then have the client remember the stuck state. They should automatically shift to the positive one. Once is enough.</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<div class='box key'><div class='box-title'>Key Takeaway</div><p><b>The floor is lava:</b> like stepping across toadstools, never lift your back foot until your front foot is down. <b>Always be touching at least one knuckle.</b> Only release the previous anchor once the next one is already firing.</p>

<p><b>You can't mess this up</b>; the only mistake is not finishing. If your hand slips or you lose track: deep breath, open eyes, close eyes, start again.</p>

</div>

## Worked example

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>Stage</th><th>What happened</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Stuck state</td><td><b>Procrastination</b>: "enough to be noteworthy". Her pattern: scrolling Instagram or cleaning the kitchen instead of putting away laundry or doing accounts.</td></tr><tr><td>End state</td><td><b>Motivation</b>: "I'd feel proud and productive"</td></tr><tr><td>Designing the middle</td><td>The client suggested frustration, then excited. The practitioner felt frustration → excitement was too big a jump. When she said "I don't know", he used <b>"If you did know, what would the answer be?"</b> (Chapter 1). They settled on <b>Determination</b>.</td></tr><tr><td>Final chain</td><td><b>Pinky</b> procrastination → frustration → determination → excitement → <b>Thumb</b> motivation</td></tr><tr><td>Anchoring</td><td>Procrastination once (no build-up); the others with the 5 → 10 ladder. "Open your eyes, close your eyes" between each, "just a state reset". Strongest: frustration and excitement.</td></tr><tr><td>Values first</td><td>Before chaining: "What would it mean to stop procrastinating?" "Great." the practitioner pushed for more: <b>Productive, fulfilled, more energised</b>.</td></tr><tr><td>Chaining</td><td>The practitioner talked her through each shift: "...the frustration leaves, and all that's left is determination..." ending with motivation built up 8 → 9 → 10, doubled, tripled.</td></tr><tr><td>The client's experience</td><td>"You could feel the negative emotions dissolving as your fingers went across the knuckles."</td></tr><tr><td>Test</td><td>Remembering another time she procrastinated: "I feel more energetic... nothing heavy or stagnant about it anymore." Felt in her chest; colour: <b>Yellow</b>.</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<div class='box extra'><div class='box-title'>Going deeper</div><p>In classic NLP this is called <b>Chaining anchors</b> (from Bandler &amp; Grinder's early work). It's used when the gap between the present state and the desired state is too big for a single collapse.</p>

<p><b>Procrastination is an emotion problem.</b> Research by Tim Pychyl and Fuschia Sirois shows procrastination is mainly about avoiding unpleasant feelings, not poor time management. Working directly with the emotion, as the bridge does, matches this.</p>

<p>"People do more to avoid pain than to gain pleasure" echoes <b>Loss aversion</b> (Kahneman &amp; Tversky): losses feel roughly twice as strong as equal gains.</p>

<p>A research-backed habit to pair with the bridge: <b>Implementation intentions</b> (Peter Gollwitzer), "<i>If</i> I notice myself scrolling instead of folding laundry, <i>then</i> I'll stand up and fold one item."</p>
