# 26. Behavioural Change: Overview & Submodalities

<p class='subtitle'>Behavioural Change: how habits work, and the building blocks of every experience</p>

<p><b>Your actions and behaviours are the clearest indicator of your values and beliefs.</b> If you want to change anything long term, you have to change what you do every day. This section covers the building blocks of behaviour, the science of habits, and three tools: <b>Submodalities</b>, <b>Like to dislike</b> and the <b>Momentum (swish) pattern</b>.</p>

## The habit loop and the NLP tools for each part

<p>From Charles Duhigg's <i>The Power of Habit</i>: every habit has three parts.</p>

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>Part</th><th>What it is</th><th>NLP tools to change it</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><b>1. Trigger</b></td><td>What creates the impulse to act</td><td>Collapse anchor · swish / momentum pattern · like to dislike · fast phobia scramble</td></tr><tr><td><b>2. Routine</b></td><td>The familiar behaviour itself (what most people call the habit)</td><td>Swish / momentum pattern · strategy scramble · chain of anchors (neural bridge)</td></tr><tr><td><b>3. Reward</b></td><td>The conscious and subconscious feedback loop that reinforces the habit and your identity</td><td>Collapse anchor · like to dislike · precision anchoring · emotional release technique · decision to destiny</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<p>Several of these tools (fast phobia scramble, strategy scramble, emotional release, decision to destiny) are covered elsewhere.</p>

## How long does a habit take?

<ul><li><b>Not 21 days, about 66 days</b> (roughly 9 weeks), according to research from University College London.</li><li>The goal is <b>Automaticity</b>: neurons wire together until the behaviour runs automatically. <b>It becomes easier to do it than not to do it</b> (momentum).</li><li>A chemical called <b>NGF</b> acts like "superglue for the brain", binding the new pathways.</li><li>He also quotes a Harvard figure: train 4 days a week for 6 months and you have a 93–95% chance of continuing for life.</li><li><b>NLP's aim is to make this process faster.</b></li></ul>

## Senses, modalities and submodalities

<p><b>Submodalities are the building blocks of experience</b>: the finer details within each representational system (Chapter 15).</p>

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>Sense</th><th>Modality</th><th>Example submodalities</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Sight</td><td><b>Visual</b> (V)</td><td>Black &amp; white or colour · near or far · bright or dim · associated or dissociated</td></tr><tr><td>Hearing</td><td><b>Auditory</b> (A)</td><td>Loud or soft · internal or external · tonality · pitch (high or low)</td></tr><tr><td>Feeling</td><td><b>Kinaesthetic</b> (K)</td><td>Intensity (0–10) · location in the body · colour · temperature · weight</td></tr><tr><td>Self-talk</td><td><b>Auditory digital</b> (Ad)</td><td>"What do you say to yourself in your own mind when you think about...?"</td></tr><tr><td>Taste / smell</td><td><b>Gustatory</b> (G) / <b>Olfactory</b> (O)</td><td>(Used less often)</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<ul><li><b>Every experience</b> (reading a book, eating a sandwich, falling in love, getting divorced, riding a bike) is a combination of modalities and their submodalities.</li><li>That "patchwork quilt" decides whether we feel something as positive or negative. <b>It's not the experience, it's how we code it.</b></li><li><b>The Instagram filter analogy:</b> filters don't change the photo, they change how it looks. Submodalities are the filters on your memories.</li><li>Everyone has a different <b>Blueprint</b> for the same thing (riding a bike). Understand someone's blueprint and you can change it, or use it to create change elsewhere.</li></ul>

## The drivers: the 20% that make 80% of the difference

<p>Like the <b>Pareto principle</b> (80/20 rule), two submodalities create most of the change. They're called <b>Drivers</b>, "the difference that makes the difference".</p>

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>Driver</th><th>Try it</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><b>1. Location</b></td><td>Picture your biggest achievement right in front of your face: intense. Now move it to the other side of the room, the country, behind your head into the past. It fades. <b>Change the location and all the other submodalities change with it.</b></td></tr><tr><td><b>2. Perspective: associated or dissociated</b></td><td>Looking through your own eyes, you <b>Relive</b> it (intense). Seeing yourself in it, you <b>Watch</b> it (less intense). Works the same way for negative memories; the fast phobia scramble even uses <b>Double dissociation</b> to create space from trauma.</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<div class='box key'><div class='box-title'>Key Takeaway</div><p>Submodalities = the building blocks (filters) of experience. <b>Drivers are always location and associated/dissociated.</b></p>

<p>Awareness precedes change: <b>Read through the checklist</b> so you know it well before the next exercises.</p>

## Subconscious reprogramming 

<p>Any submodality technique is a kind of <b>Pattern interrupt</b>: it breaks the existing link between a trigger and its response, and at the same time creates a positive link to a new behaviour, state or belief. Coding something differently builds new neural pathways and new choices.</p>

### The general process

<ol><li>Decide the <b>Unwanted</b> state or behaviour.</li><li>Choose the <b>Preferred</b> one to replace it.</li><li>Elicit the internal representation (IR) and submodalities of <b>Both</b>.</li><li>Find the <b>Drivers</b>: the key submodalities that differ.</li><li>Change the unwanted one's submodalities to match the preferred one's.</li><li><b>Test</b> and link the new response to the future.</li></ol>

<p><b>Keys to success:</b> <b>Speed</b> (so it stays unconscious, not a slow conscious process) · use universally generalised experiences · find the drivers.</p>

### Intervention script notes (used with every change technique)

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>When</th><th>Ask</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Getting the presenting problem</td><td><b>"How is this a problem for you now?"</b> (makes sure you're dealing with the real issue, not just a symptom)</td></tr><tr><td>Before the intervention</td><td><b>"Is it okay with your unconscious mind for you to release this problem and have conscious awareness of it?"</b> Get a congruent <b>Yes</b> before going on.</td></tr><tr><td>After the intervention</td><td><b>"How do you feel?"</b> (wait) <b>"How do you know?"</b></td></tr><tr><td>Future pace</td><td><b>"Step out into some indefinite point in the future where, in the past, this old problem would have held you back, and notice how you're showing up differently now. How are you acting and behaving differently?"</b></td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<p>The main techniques in this area are: <b>Submodality elicitation, momentum pattern, response repatterning (like to dislike) and belief change</b>.</p>

</div>

<div class='box extra'><div class='box-title'>Going deeper</div><p><b>Submodalities</b> were developed by Richard Bandler; the classic book is <i>Using Your Brain for a Change</i> (1985).</p>

<p>Research backs up the dissociation driver: <b>Self-distancing</b> studies (Ethan Kross &amp; Ozlem Ayduk) found that recalling a painful memory from a third-person, "fly on the wall" view reduced emotional distress compared with reliving it.</p>

<p><b>Psychological distance</b> (construal level theory): things imagined as far away in space or time feel less intense and more abstract, which fits the location driver.</p>

<p>James Clear's <i>Atomic Habits</i> (2018) extends Duhigg's loop to four steps: <b>Cue, craving, response, reward</b>.</p>
