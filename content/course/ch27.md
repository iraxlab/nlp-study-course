# 27. Like to Dislike (Response Repatterning)

<p class='subtitle'>Behavioural Change: turning a craving into a dislike</p>

<p>A like to dislike changes the <b>Underlying associations</b> you have with food, drinks, behaviours or anything else. Anything you like can become something you don't, and vice versa (<b>Dislike to like</b> is the same process in reverse). It's very effective with food and drink because taste is such a visceral experience.</p>

<p>In NLP this is called <b>Response repatterning</b>: unlocking how someone codes and stores their response at the unconscious level, then changing it to open up new choices. It can be used on:</p>

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>Food</th><th>People</th><th>Projects</th><th>Behaviour</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Develop an intense distaste for coffee, red wine or ice cream</td><td>Change your response to a colleague and their quirks</td><td>Get moving on delayed projects</td><td>Develop a compelling desire to exercise</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<p>It's also the <b>Set-up exercise for belief change</b>, taught later.</p>

## It happens naturally

<ul><li>Drink too much of something, get violently ill, and you can't touch it again (tequila, black sambuca).</li><li>Some people find anchovies disgusting and love coriander. Some people are the opposite. "These are all just taste buds," and they can be changed.</li></ul>

## Key rules

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>Rule</th><th>Explanation</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><b>Specificity is key</b></td><td>Not "sugar", "takeaway" or "carbs" (the brain can't associate to a whole category, and sugar is in everything). Pick a specific item: your go-to chocolate, a specific drink. Chocolate as a category is okay. Because the brain is like a spiderweb, changing one item often changes related ones.</td></tr><tr><td><b>Same logical type</b></td><td>Solid for solid, liquid for liquid; something with most of the same qualities. Examples: <b>Coffee → castor oil</b>, <b>Ice cream → spoiled lumpy milk</b>.</td></tr><tr><td><b>The more disgusting, the better</b></td><td>It should produce the biggest negative response. Anything that has been in the client's mouth or touched their lips works.</td></tr><tr><td><b>Confirm 100% commitment</b></td><td>"The two keys to lasting change are <b>Commitment</b> and <b>Conditioning</b>." If they're committed, the exercise just helps; if they're not, no exercise will work.</td></tr><tr><td><b>Go fast</b></td><td>Speed keeps it an unconscious process.</td></tr><tr><td><b>Focus on the drivers</b></td><td>Many submodalities will be the same for both foods; that's fine. Location and associated/dissociated matter most.</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

## The steps

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>#</th><th>Step</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>1</td><td>Find a food or drink you <b>Like but wish you didn't</b> (one you overindulge in).</td></tr><tr><td>2</td><td>Find one you <b>Absolutely hate</b>, of a similar logical type. <b>Confirm commitment</b>.</td></tr><tr><td>3</td><td>Elicit the <b>Submodalities of the "like"</b> food (checklist, Chapter 26).</td></tr><tr><td>4</td><td><b>Break state</b> ("clear the screen").</td></tr><tr><td>5</td><td>Elicit the <b>Submodalities of the "dislike"</b> food.</td></tr><tr><td>6</td><td><b>Break state.</b></td></tr><tr><td>7</td><td><b>Map across</b>: give the "like" food the "dislike" food's submodalities (especially location and association).</td></tr><tr><td>8</td><td><b>Lock it in</b> with the Tupperware sound: "Thwack!"</td></tr><tr><td>9</td><td><b>Break state.</b></td></tr><tr><td>10</td><td><b>Test</b> and future pace.</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

### The script 

<div class='script'><div class='script-row'><b>Ecology:</b> "Is it okay with your unconscious mind to make this change today and have conscious awareness of it?"</div><div class='script-row'><b>Like:</b> "What's a food that you like but wish you didn't?" ... "As you think about that food that you like, and how you know you like it, do you have a picture?" → elicit submodalities ("Is it black and white or colour?...")</div><div class='script-row'><b>Clear the screen.</b></div><div class='script-row'><b>Dislike:</b> "What's a food of a similar logical type that you absolutely hate?" ... "Think of the thing you absolutely hate and how you know you hate it. Have you got a picture?" → elicit submodalities</div><div class='script-row'><b>Clear the screen.</b></div><div class='script-row'><b>Map across:</b> "Now think of the food you like... take that image you have stored in your mind and see that it becomes... the sounds you hear become... the feelings you feel now become... and you say to yourself in your own mind... Go ahead and do that now."</div><div class='script-row'><b>Lock:</b> "You know the sound Tupperware makes? <b>Thwack!</b> Lock it into place."</div><div class='script-row'><b>Break state:</b> "Do you smell popcorn?"</div><div class='script-row'><b>Test:</b> "Now how do you feel about [the food]?" · "The next time you have a chance to have ___, what do you do?"</div></div>

## Worked example: salt and vinegar chips

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th></th><th>LIKE: salt &amp; vinegar chips (crinkle cut)</th><th>DISLIKE: scabs / dry crusty skin</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Colour</td><td>Colour</td><td>Colour (brown and crusty)</td></tr><tr><td>Near or far / location</td><td><b>Close, right there in front</b></td><td><b>Down below</b></td></tr><tr><td>Brightness</td><td>Bright</td><td></td></tr><tr><td>Associated?</td><td>Associated (eating them herself)</td><td>Associated</td></tr><tr><td>Sounds</td><td>The packet and the crunch; loud</td><td></td></tr><tr><td>Self-talk</td><td>"Oh my god, yum."</td><td>"Gross, why am I doing this?"</td></tr><tr><td>Feelings</td><td>A tingle on the lips, 10/10</td><td>Dry retching</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<ul><li><b>Choosing the dislike:</b> she suggested rotten eggs, then toenails; the practitioner steered towards something <b>Similar to chips</b> (crunchy, flaky). She chose dry, crusty skin from her heels.</li><li><b>Mapping:</b> the practitioner moved the chips picture <b>Down below</b> to where the scabs were (location driver), then told the story: reaching into the familiar red and pink chip bag... it's a <b>Bag of scabs</b>; "that's gross"; an insatiable urge to dry retch, yet you keep eating; turn it up to 10. Then <b>Tupperware lock</b>.</li><li><b>Test:</b> eyes open, "What do you think about salt and vinegar chips?" the practitioner offered her a real bag. She didn't want any.</li></ul>

## Choosing your two foods

<p>Write two lists:</p>

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>List 1: like but wish you didn't</th><th>List 2: absolutely despise</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>A food or drink you overindulge in; your "Achilles heel". If you could click your heels like Dorothy in <i>The Wizard of Oz</i> and stop, it would transform your health and quality of life.</td><td>A food or drink you can't stomach, or can't think about without feeling physically ill.</td></tr><tr><td><i>Common answers:</i> Pepsi Max, milk, peanut butter, bread, chocolate (lots of it), wine, soft drinks, rice, cigarettes, tea with milk, sweets, chips, cheese, coffee, cakes, ice cream</td><td><i>Common answers:</i> tequila, black sambuca, Jägermeister, whiskey, boiled broccoli, mushrooms, avocado, peas, organ meats, black pudding, durian, kale, anything pickled, Turkish delight</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<ul><li><b>The "dislike" matters more than the "like":</b> the more extreme the dislike, the better it works.</li><li><b>"I don't hate any food"?</b> There's still a <b>Spectrum</b>. Rank every food from most to least exciting and there's always a least favourite. Something you really dislike is better, though.</li><li><b>Similar logical type</b>: liquid for liquid, solid for solid.</li><li>It must be something you've <b>Actually tasted</b> (touched your lips), though not necessarily recently.</li></ul>

## Worked example: bread

<p><b>The client's foods:</b> <b>Bread</b> (her family gets through about a loaf and a half a day) and <b>Tongue</b>. She once worked in the offal section of a meatworks, where a rotten tongue came through and spewed over the others being washed.</p>

<p><b>Why it works:</b> she likes bread and dislikes tongue not because of bread or tongue, but because of her <b>Blueprint</b>: the images, sounds, feelings and self-talk she attaches to each. The practitioner focuses on the <b>Four primary systems (V, A, K, Ad)</b>; taste and smell matter less, even with food. Then he puts "the filters of tongue on the image of bread".</p>

<p><b>Motivation first:</b> "What would it mean to stop eating bread?" She once lost 5 kg in a week without it; she'd feel lighter, more comfortable in her body, and closer to her partner. Wanting tongue? "Negative 102."</p>

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th></th><th>BREAD (like)</th><th>TONGUE (dislike)</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Colour</td><td>Colour</td><td>Colour</td></tr><tr><td>Near / far, location</td><td>Near, <b>Right in front of her face</b></td><td>Near, in front but not in her face; <b>Big and centred</b></td></tr><tr><td>Associated?</td><td><b>Associated</b></td><td><b>Dissociated</b></td></tr><tr><td>Framed / panoramic</td><td>Panoramic</td><td>Panoramic</td></tr><tr><td>Movie / still</td><td>Movie</td><td>Movie, fast</td></tr><tr><td>Brightness</td><td>Bright</td><td>Bright</td></tr><tr><td>Sounds</td><td>The packet opening: loud, internal</td><td>Spewing and running water</td></tr><tr><td>Feelings</td><td>Happy 7/10 (orange) and disgusted 9/10 (plum purple)</td><td>Sickly, gagging, throat choked, can't swallow</td></tr><tr><td>Self-talk</td><td>"Really? Again?" "I don't need it, it's more of a want."</td><td>"Don't. Just don't."</td></tr><tr><td>Smell</td><td></td><td>Putrid</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<p><b>Pattern interrupt:</b> between the two foods, the practitioner broke state with a deliberately random, cheeky question ("Are you wearing underwear?"), so nobody was still thinking about bread. Any unexpected question works.</p>

<p><b>Mapping across:</b> bread in full colour, laid right on top of the tongue image until they blend; a loaf of chopped-up tongues; opening the bag and being hit by the putrid smell. Then <b>Step out</b> (dissociate), <b>Move it further away</b>, make it <b>Big and central</b> like the tongue; the gagging, choking feeling; "don't, just don't"; spew sounds. <b>Tupperware lock</b>, deep breaths.</p>

<p><b>Test:</b> "What's the first thing that comes to mind when you think about opening a packet of bread?" the client: <b>"Get it away from me."</b></p>

<p>Extra points: "It doesn't really matter if you mess it up, it's only food and drink," but learn it properly because <b>Belief change uses the same process</b>. Follow along with the <b>Submodality checklist in this chapter</b> and write down the example answers.</p>

<div class='box extra'><div class='box-title'>Going deeper</div><p>A one-off bad experience with a drink is a textbook <b>Conditioned taste aversion</b> (the <b>Garcia effect</b>, John Garcia, 1955): a single episode of feeling ill after a food can create a lasting aversion to it, much faster than most learning.</p>

<p>Using imagined disgust to reduce a habit is similar to <b>Covert sensitisation</b> (Joseph Cautela, 1960s), a behavioural therapy technique in which an unwanted behaviour is paired in imagination with an unpleasant scene.</p>

<p>Food cravings rely heavily on <b>Mental imagery</b> (Kavanagh's elaborated intrusion theory). Studies show that changing or occupying visual imagery (e.g. playing a visual game) reduces craving strength, which supports changing the picture.</p>

<p>The <b>Tupperware "thwack"</b> is an auditory anchor (Chapter 21) that "seals" the change.</p>
