# 28. Momentum Pattern (Swish Pattern)

<p class='subtitle'>Behavioural Change: catching the trigger and swapping the old movie for a new one</p>

<p><b>Momentum pattern</b> and <b>Swish pattern</b> are the same technique; the name depends on the NLP school. It changes behaviour at the <b>Trigger</b>: when someone is triggered, they normally head down an undesired path. The swish catches them at that moment and points them in a new direction.</p>

## When to use it

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>Use it for (mild)</th><th>Not for (severe)</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Road rage when someone cuts you off · coming home to a messy house and snapping at the kids · arguing with your partner · other small, undesired (not "bad") behaviours</td><td>Extreme behaviours such as <b>Self-harm</b>. These need proper professional support.</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

## The computer screen metaphor

<p>Imagine one window open on a computer. Click <b>Minimise</b> and it drops away; click another and it <b>Maximises</b> to full screen. The swish does that with two "movies":</p>

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>Old movie (minimise)</th><th>New movie (maximise)</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>You at the trigger point doing the old behaviour (seeing the mess, snapping, growling)</td><td>The best version of you at the same trigger (deep breaths, hug the kids, get changed, reset, music on, a great evening)</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<p>Over many repetitions this <b>Rewires the nervous system</b> so the trigger leads straight to the new behaviour, creating <b>Momentum</b>.</p>

## The steps

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>Step</th><th>What to do</th><th>Key question</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><b>1. Undesired behaviour</b></td><td>Identify a behaviour the client is committed to changing</td><td>What do you do now?</td></tr><tr><td><b>2. Trigger</b></td><td>Find the trigger: something seen, heard, felt or said to themselves</td><td><b>"What has to happen in order for you to [behaviour]?"</b></td></tr><tr><td><b>3. Ideal outcome</b></td><td>Elicit what they'd like to do instead, and "juice it up"</td><td><b>"What would the best version of you do in this situation?"</b></td></tr><tr><td><b>4. Rapid installation</b></td><td>Swish from old to new with a hand gesture and sound, many times, faster each time</td><td></td></tr><tr><td><b>5. Break state and test</b></td><td>When they can barely see the old picture ("smoke coming out of their ears"), break state, test and future pace</td><td>"Next time you [trigger], what happens?"</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

### How to deliver the swish

<ul><li><b>Eyes open</b>, with the client sitting next to you, visualising in front of them.</li><li><b>Hand gesture:</b> move your hand from the <b>Bottom left</b> of their visual field to the <b>Top right</b> (one version says lower left to the centre in front of their face).</li><li><b>Sound:</b> "<b>Whsssht!</b>", an auditory anchor that gives the pattern its name.</li><li><b>Repetitions:</b> about <b>15–30</b> (chapter) or around <b>50</b> (chips example).</li><li><b>Start slow</b> with lots of detail; each time, <b>Less language and more speed</b>. By the end you're just gesturing. More repetitions = more powerful.</li><li><b>Read the script</b>: "use the tools, don't be a tool."</li></ul>

## The steps and script 

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th>#</th><th>Step</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>1</td><td>Elicit the unwanted behaviour: have them describe it</td></tr><tr><td>2</td><td>Find the trigger for the behaviour, state or belief</td></tr><tr><td>3</td><td>Elicit the IR (picture) they're accessing at the trigger</td></tr><tr><td>4</td><td>Break state, eyes open</td></tr><tr><td>5</td><td>Elicit the desired behaviour</td></tr><tr><td>6</td><td>Get the IR of the preferred behaviour</td></tr><tr><td>7</td><td>Have them <b>Associate</b> into it and <b>Intensify</b> submodalities so it's compelling</td></tr><tr><td>8</td><td>Have them <b>Dissociate</b> from it (see the ideal you in the picture)</td></tr><tr><td>9</td><td>Break state</td></tr><tr><td>10</td><td>Have them associate back into the old trigger picture</td></tr><tr><td>11</td><td>Place the new picture <b>Small and dark in the lower-left corner</b></td></tr><tr><td>12</td><td>Swish: hand from lower left to the centre, "Whsssht!"; the new picture explodes big and bright while the old one shrinks into the corner</td></tr><tr><td>13</td><td>Break state</td></tr><tr><td>14</td><td>Repeat 10–13 faster and faster, dropping language each time, breaking state between each, until they can't experience the old behaviour</td></tr><tr><td>15</td><td>Break state, test and future pace</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<div class='script'><div class='script-row'>"<b>How do you know it's time to ___?</b>" ... "Close your eyes. When you think of that [trigger] now, do you have a picture?" → elicit submodalities. "Okay, good. Now open your eyes."</div><div class='script-row'>"<b>How would you like to act instead?</b>" ... "When you think of that behaviour, do you have a picture?" → elicit submodalities.</div><div class='script-row'>"We're just going to adjust some of the qualities of the picture now. Step into it, see it through your own eyes. If it's black and white, make it colour. Turn the brightness up, so it's most compelling and realistic for you. Turn the volume up on the sounds. Notice the feelings and <b>Double</b> their intensity now."</div><div class='script-row'>"Now step out of the picture so you see yourself, the ideal you, in the picture." <b>Clear the screen.</b></div><div class='script-row'>"Close your eyes. Take the picture of [the old trigger] and put it back on the screen, looking through your own eyes. As you look at it, have the new picture of the ideal you appear <b>Small and dark in the lower left-hand corner</b>."</div><div class='script-row'>"In a moment, not yet, I'll have you replace the old picture with the new one. When I say '<b>Whsssht!</b>' the new picture will <b>Explode big and bright</b> on the screen of your mind, while the old one fades and shrinks into the lower left corner. Do that now, quickly! <b>WHSSSHT!</b>"</div><div class='script-row'>"Clear the screen" / "Open your eyes." (Repeat, faster.)</div><div class='script-row'><b>Test:</b> "So now when you think about the next time you [experience the old trigger], what happens? What do you do?"</div></div>

<div class='box key'><div class='box-title'>Key Takeaway</div><p>Two movies, one trigger: <b>Minimise the old, maximise the new</b>. Start slow and detailed, get faster with fewer words, gesture every time, then break state and test.</p>

<p>Mild behaviours only. The magic question: <b>"What would the best version of you do in this situation?"</b></p>

</div>

## Worked example: the client and the messy house

<div class='table-wrap'><table><thead><tr><th></th><th>Old movie</th><th>New movie</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Trigger</td><td>Walking through the door: kids' shoes, uniforms and lunch boxes on the floor</td><td>The same door, the same mess</td></tr><tr><td>Behaviour</td><td>"The look" and having a go at her husband</td><td>A big smile; sneaking up to hug and kiss her husband; asking about his day</td></tr><tr><td>Picture</td><td>Colour, a movie, no important sounds</td><td>Bright, brilliant, everything turned up to 10</td></tr><tr><td>Feeling</td><td>"In control"</td><td>Heart <b>Overjoyed</b>, coloured <b>Red</b>; feeling "so special"</td></tr><tr><td>Others</td><td></td><td>Husband, surprised: "My day was wonderful. How was yours?" Kids (10, 8 and 6) run in for hugs and kisses</td></tr><tr><td>Self-talk</td><td></td><td>"<b>I love me.</b>"</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

<ul><li>The practitioner had her "take a photo" of the new scene, <b>Step out</b> of it, and want to be that woman.</li><li>Both images placed in the bottom left; then swish after swish, the language shrinking each time to just "walking through the door, husband, kisses, love, cuddles, red... clear the screen."</li><li><b>Future pace:</b> coming home in the future and seeing the mess, what comes to mind? "My husband sitting at the table... he's continuing his work so he can get it done, so he can sit on the lounge after dinner and cuddle." The <b>Same scene now has a loving meaning.</b></li></ul>

<p>Bottom left → top right using <b>Eye accessing cues</b> (bottom left = feelings, top right = constructed images). As noted in Chapter 15, research hasn't supported eye-movement cues, so treat that explanation as NLP theory rather than science.</p>

<p>The number of repetitions varies: classic NLP uses about 5, some trainers use 15–30 and some go to around 50.</p>

<div class='box extra'><div class='box-title'>Going deeper</div><p>The swish pattern was developed by <b>Richard Bandler</b> and described in <i>Using Your Brain for a Change</i> (1985). The classic version uses size and brightness: the cue picture is big and bright, the new self-image small and dark, and they swap.</p>

<p>Research on the swish itself is limited, but its ingredients line up with well-supported tools: <b>Implementation intentions</b> ("If [trigger], then I will [new behaviour]", Gollwitzer) and <b>Mental contrasting</b> (Gabriele Oettingen's WOOP: Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan).</p>

<p>Making the new self-image <b>Dissociated</b> (seeing yourself) gives you something to move <b>Towards</b>, which NLP says makes it more motivating.</p>

<p>Imagery rescripting, used in CBT for unwanted images, also works by replacing an old mental picture with a new one.</p>

</div>
